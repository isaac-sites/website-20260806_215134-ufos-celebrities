## Introduction

The best-known videos of the [Phoenix Lights]({{ 'phoenix-lights/' | relative_url }}) do not show the earlier V-shaped formation reported across Arizona during the evening of 13 March 1997. Instead, they largely record a separate display that appeared around 10 p.m. over the south-western horizon near Phoenix. This distinction is crucial because the visual behaviour captured on video closely matches military illumination flares released on parachutes, whereas the earlier reports remain the more disputed part of the broader Phoenix Lights story. Understanding what the famous recordings actually depict helps explain why investigators often reach different conclusions depending on which part of the evening they are analysing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2016/11/the-phoenix-lights-become-an-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

## What the 10 p.m. recordings actually show

Most widely circulated footage shows a row or gentle arc of bright orange-white lights suspended low above the south-western horizon. Rather than travelling rapidly across the sky, the lights appear almost stationary for several minutes before disappearing individually.

## How parachute flares appear to hover

A common objection is that the lights seemed to remain fixed in one position rather than falling. However, this is expected behaviour when powerful illumination flares are viewed from many kilometres away.

LUU-2 illumination flares are designed to descend slowly beneath parachutes while providing sustained battlefield lighting. Their rate of descent is modest, and at long distances the downward movement can be difficult to detect because most of the motion is along the observer's line of sight rather than across it. The combination of slow descent, great distance and intense brightness can therefore create the impression that the lights are hovering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

Several additional visual factors reinforce this illusion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Distance compresses movement.** A flare descending hundreds of metres may appear almost motionless when viewed from dozens of kilometres away.
* **Night removes depth cues.** Without visible terrain, observers cannot easily judge altitude or speed.
* **Brightness dominates perception.** The flare itself is visible, while its parachute and any smoke are usually too faint to see at such ranges.
* **Atmospheric effects.** Heat shimmer and changing air conditions can make the lights flicker or appear to pulse without indicating unusual motion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

These optical effects explain why many witnesses sincerely described stationary lights even though the objects were slowly descending.

## Why the lights vanished in sequence

Perhaps the most striking feature of the famous recordings is that the lights seem to switch off one after another.

Early interpretations suggested that each light extinguished independently or that a single enormous craft was somehow turning off its lights. Detailed video analysis offered a different explanation.

Researchers compared the original night footage with daytime photographs taken from the same camera positions. When the landscape was visible, the apparent disappearance points aligned closely with the crest of the Sierra Estrella mountain range south-west of Phoenix. As each descending flare passed behind progressively higher parts of the ridge, it became hidden from view while continuing to burn. Because the flares had been released at slightly different positions and times, they disappeared sequentially rather than simultaneously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[skepticalinquirer.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

This explanation accounts for several otherwise puzzling features visible in the recordings:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the lights remain in approximately the same horizontal arrangement;
* they disappear from one side of the formation to the other;
* none shows sudden acceleration or manoeuvring before vanishing;
* the timing is consistent with slow descent behind uneven terrain rather than instantaneous extinction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

## Why the mountain-ridge explanation became influential

The mountain-ridge interpretation gained credibility because it generated a testable prediction rather than relying only on verbal descriptions.

Image analysts aligned video frames with daytime reference photographs from the same viewpoints. If the lights were descending flares beyond the mountains, each disappearance should coincide with the skyline. Independent analyses found that this is exactly what occurred. Local television demonstrations also filmed known flare drops over the same range, producing remarkably similar visual behaviour to the famous 10 p.m. recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

The explanation therefore rests on multiple mutually supporting observations:

## What this explanation does—and does not—address

The [flare explanation]({{ 'evidence-limits-071e7a/' | relative_url }}) is strongest when applied specifically to the later 10 p.m. recordings.

Many researchers, including some who remain interested in the earlier reports, distinguish between these two phases of the evening. The evidence supporting parachute flares is directed at the stationary horizon lights captured on video, not necessarily at every witness account from earlier in the night. This distinction explains why debates over the Phoenix Lights often persist: participants are sometimes discussing different events while using the same name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2016/11/the-phoenix-lights-become-an-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

Consequently, the famous videos themselves are widely regarded by sceptical investigators and many independent analysts as being consistent with military flare drops viewed over the Sierra Estrella mountains. The continuing controversy surrounding the Phoenix Lights largely centres on whether the earlier moving formation reported between roughly 8 p.m. and 9 p.m. represents a separate phenomenon requiring a different explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2016/11/the-phoenix-lights-become-an-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>
