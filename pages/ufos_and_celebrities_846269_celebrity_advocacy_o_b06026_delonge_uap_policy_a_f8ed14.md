## Introduction

Tom DeLonge did not persuade the US government to treat unidentified aerial phenomena (UAPs) as a policy issue on his own. His significance lies in the way he connected celebrity visibility with the credibility of former defence and intelligence officials, helping move discussion away from popular culture and towards questions of aviation safety, intelligence collection and government accountability. Rather than convincing institutions to embrace extraordinary explanations, DeLonge helped create a public platform where military personnel, former officials and journalists could present UAP incidents as matters worthy of official investigation. ## Building a media-facing team of former officials

The defining feature of DeLonge's approach was not simply that he spoke publicly about UFOs. Many entertainers had done that for decades with little institutional effect. His innovation was to establish **[To The Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) of Arts & Science (TTSA)** in 2017 as a hybrid organisation combining entertainment, research and former government expertise. The founding team included former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Intelligence Christopher Mellon, former intelligence officer Jim Semivan, physicist Hal Puthoff and Luis Elizondo, who said he had overseen the Pentagon's Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_The_Stars_Inc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">To The Stars Inc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">To The Stars Inc</span></span></span>

This composition mattered because it changed who was delivering the message. DeLonge attracted audiences that defence officials alone would rarely reach, while Mellon and Elizondo translated unusual aerial encounters into language familiar to policymakers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* restricted military airspace;
* pilot safety;
* sensor performance;
* intelligence collection;
* national security risk.

</div>

That combination broadened the audience beyond UFO enthusiasts. Journalists covering defence, Congress and national security increasingly treated the issue as one involving military reporting standards rather than folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. ## Turning military footage into mainstream news

The most important moment in DeLonge's policy influence came in December 2017.

On the same day that *The New York Times* revealed the existence of the Pentagon's AATIP programme, TTSA published Navy cockpit videos that became widely known as **FLIR1**, **Gimbal** and, later, **GoFast**. The simultaneous appearance of investigative reporting and military imagery transformed public discussion. Instead of relying primarily on eyewitness stories, journalists and policymakers were examining authenticated military sensor recordings connected to trained aviators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. The videos themselves did not establish what the observed objects were. Subsequent reporting highlighted unresolved questions about their release process and noted that the available footage lacked sufficient contextual data to determine the nature of the objects. Nevertheless, the recordings achieved something politically significant: they reframed unidentified objects as documented military incidents rather than anecdotal civilian claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. The US Navy later confirmed that the videos depicted genuine **unidentified aerial phenomena**, carefully avoiding any claim about extraterrestrial origin. That official terminology further reinforced the shift away from sensational language towards operational reporting and risk assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/navy-confirms-ufo-videos-real" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy Confirms UFO Videos Are Real and Show Unidentified Aerial Phenomena &#124; HISTORY...</span></span></span>

## How publicity became policy attention

Celebrity attention alone rarely produces lasting policy change. DeLonge's influence worked because it intersected with existing institutional concerns.

Several mechanisms helped convert publicity into official attention:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Agenda setting.** High-profile media coverage encouraged mainstream news organisations to devote sustained attention to military UAP reports rather than treating them as entertainment stories.
* **Credible intermediaries.** Former officials such as Mellon and Elizondo could brief journalists and legislators using defence terminology and institutional experience.
* **Political framing.** Rather than arguing that aliens had been proven, advocates increasingly focused on unidentified objects operating near military training areas and sensitive installations.
* **Public accountability.** Questions shifted towards whether reporting systems were adequate and whether Congress had sufficient oversight of government investigations.

</div>

This reframing lowered the political cost of engaging with the issue. Members of Congress could ask whether unidentified objects represented foreign surveillance technology, drones, sensor anomalies or other aviation hazards without endorsing extraordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.axios.com/2019/04/27/navy-reports-increase-ufo-sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: axios.com">[Axios]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">axios.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Navy reports increase in UFO sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy has reported an increase in UFO sightings, particularly near sensitive military zones, following the establishment of the Space Forc...</span></span></span>

## Where celebrity influence ended and institutional action began

It is important not to overstate DeLonge's role.

Official developments—including revised Navy reporting procedures, congressional briefings, intelligence assessments and later creation of dedicated Department of Defense UAP offices—were governmental decisions responding to multiple pressures. Military personnel had been reporting unusual encounters independently, investigative journalists uncovered important documentation, and lawmakers pursued oversight through established legislative processes. DeLonge neither controlled nor directed those institutional actions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.axios.com/2019/04/27/navy-reports-increase-ufo-sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: axios.com">[Axios]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">axios.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Navy reports increase in UFO sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy has reported an increase in UFO sightings, particularly near sensitive military zones, following the establishment of the Space Forc...</span></span></span>

DeLonge helped elevate the subject onto the policy agenda; the subsequent investigations remained governed by military, intelligence and scientific standards rather than celebrity narratives.

## The lasting "DeLonge effect"

The lasting significance of DeLonge's campaign lies less in any specific claim than in the communication model it pioneered. By combining celebrity reach with former national security officials and strategically releasing military-related material through mainstream journalism, TTSA demonstrated that UAPs could be discussed as questions of public administration rather than solely as paranormal entertainment.

That communication strategy altered the public conversation in three enduring ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it normalised discussion of UAPs within mainstream political reporting;
* it encouraged policymakers to frame unidentified objects as potential aviation and intelligence issues;
* it separated the question of whether incidents deserved investigation from the far more contentious question of what ultimately caused them.

</div>

For that reason, DeLonge's contribution is best understood as a mechanism of agenda-setting rather than proof of any particular explanation for UAP reports.
