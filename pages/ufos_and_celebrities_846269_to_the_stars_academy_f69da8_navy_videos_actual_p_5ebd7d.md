## Introduction

The three infrared videos known as **FLIR1**, **Gimbal** and **Go Fast** became the most influential public contribution associated with [To the Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) (TTSA). By helping bring the recordings into public view in 2017 and 2018, TTSA shifted discussion of unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) from niche UFO circles into mainstream news, military reporting and eventually congressional attention. What made the videos significant, however, was not proof of extraordinary technology. Their importance lay in a narrower but still notable fact: the US Navy and later the Department of Defense confirmed that the recordings were authentic military footage depicting objects or sensor tracks that had not been identified at the time of the encounters. That official authentication established the origin of the videos, not the nature of the objects they showed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

Understanding that distinction is essential. Authentic military footage can still have multiple [possible explanations]({{ 'possible-explanations/' | relative_url }}), particularly when only short infrared clips are available without the complete radar data, flight logs, electronic sensor information and classified contextual material that investigators normally use to identify aircraft or other objects.

## How TTSA introduced FLIR1, Gimbal and Go Fast

TTSA did not create the recordings. The footage originated from US Navy aircraft during separate incidents in 2004 and 2015.

## What official authentication actually confirmed

A common misunderstanding is that the Navy "confirmed UFOs" or "confirmed alien spacecraft." That is not what happened.

In 2019, Navy spokesperson Joseph Gradisher confirmed that the three circulating videos were genuine Navy recordings and stated that the Navy regarded the phenomena depicted as **unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP)**. Importantly, he also said the Navy had not released any official hypothesis or conclusion about what the objects were.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/u-s-navy-confirms-videos-depict-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-not-cleared-for-public-release/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultU.S. Navy Confirms Videos Depict ‘Unidentified Aerial Phenomena’; Not Cleared For Public Release - The Black Vault...</span></span></span>

The distinction became even clearer in April 2020 when the Department of Defense officially released the videos. According to the Pentagon, the release was intended to dispel public confusion over whether the circulating footage was authentic. The Department stated that the aerial phenomena shown in the videos remained characterised as unidentified, but it did not attribute them to extraterrestrial technology, advanced secret aircraft or any other specific cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosurearchives.com/events/dod-releases-three-videos-2020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosurearchives.com">[Disclosure Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosurearchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Disclosure ArchivesDepartment of Defense officially releases three Navy UAP videos · Disclosure ArchivesApril 27, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 27, 2020</span></span></span>

Official confirmation therefore established three limited points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the videos were authentic military recordings;
* they had been recorded by Navy aircraft during genuine operations;
* investigators had not identified the observed objects at the time represented by the released footage.

</div>

None of those findings established what the objects ultimately were.

## Why the videos alone cannot determine what was observed

The public versions of FLIR1, Gimbal and Go Fast are very short infrared clips extracted from much larger military sensor systems.

Military aircraft typically collect multiple streams of information simultaneously, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* airborne radar tracks;
* infrared imagery;
* aircraft position and attitude;
* pilot communications;
* targeting system telemetry;
* supporting radar from ships or airborne warning aircraft.

</div>

Only fragments of that information have been released publicly. Without the complete sensor picture, outside researchers must infer critical quantities such as object distance, altitude, speed and direction from incomplete data.

That limitation matters because apparent extraordinary motion can sometimes result from ordinary geometry. Changes in aircraft heading, zoom settings, sensor stabilisation, viewing angle and parallax can produce visual impressions that differ from an object's actual flight path. Conversely, incomplete public footage also means it cannot be ruled out that additional classified information might support unusual observations reported by military personnel. The released clips alone cannot resolve that question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Gimbal and Go Fast remain debated

The continuing debate is not primarily about whether the videos are genuine—they are—but about how they should be interpreted.

For **Gimbal**, one recurring discussion concerns the apparent rotation of the object. Several optical analyses have suggested that some or all of the visible rotation could result from the infrared targeting pod's image-processing and camera mechanics rather than the object physically rotating in flight. Other researchers argue that eyewitness testimony and additional sensor information described by participating aviators are more consistent with genuinely unusual behaviour than with a simple imaging artefact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reconstruction of Potential Flight Paths for the January 2015 Gimbal UAP...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 2015</span></span></span>

For **Go Fast**, the object appears to race across the ocean surface at remarkable speed. However, analyses using the displayed instrument data have argued that, depending on the object's range from the aircraft, much of the apparent speed may result from parallax and the motion of the observing aircraft rather than extreme velocity close to the water. Again, without the underlying classified sensor information, no definitive public conclusion is possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

The released footage therefore supports multiple technically plausible interpretations, which is why it continues to be studied.

## What the videos changed—and what they did not

The Navy videos transformed public discussion because they established that military personnel had encountered events considered worthy of documentation and investigation. Before their official authentication, many observers dismissed the recordings as unverifiable internet material. After the Navy and Department of Defense acknowledged their [authenticity]({{ 'authenticity/' | relative_url }}), that position became much harder to sustain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosurearchives.com/events/dod-releases-three-videos-2020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosurearchives.com">[Disclosure Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosurearchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Disclosure ArchivesDepartment of Defense officially releases three Navy UAP videos · Disclosure ArchivesApril 27, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 27, 2020</span></span></span>

At the same time, authentication did not validate every claim made around the videos. It did not prove:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* extraterrestrial visitors;
* breakthrough propulsion systems;
* impossible physics;
* secret government knowledge of alien technology.

</div>

Instead, the official position remained deliberately narrow: the recordings were genuine military footage of aerial phenomena that had not been identified based on the available operational information released to the public. That distinction explains why the videos became both highly influential and persistently controversial.

For TTSA, this represented its clearest public success. The organisation helped move authentic military evidence into mainstream debate. Yet the strongest official finding supported by the videos was also the most limited: they demonstrated that trained military personnel recorded phenomena they could not immediately identify—not that the recordings resolved what those phenomena ultimately were.
