## Introduction

Reports of unidentified lights are disproportionately common at night because darkness removes many of the visual cues that people normally rely on to judge distance, size and motion. A single bright light against a featureless sky provides very little information about how far away it is, whether it is moving, or even whether it is large or small. As a result, witnesses may sincerely perceive a light as accelerating, hovering unnaturally or changing direction when the available visual information is too limited to support reliable estimates. This does not mean that every night-time UFO report has an ordinary explanation, but it does mean that darkness significantly increases the risk of perceptual error. NASA's review of unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) similarly notes that establishing reliable distance is essential before extraordinary claims about speed or manoeuvrability can be evaluated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. ## Depth cues disappear after dark

During daylight, the human visual system constantly estimates distance using familiar landmarks, surface textures, shadows, perspective and the visible horizon. At night, many of these cues vanish.

An isolated point of light offers almost none of the information needed to determine its range. A nearby drone, a distant aircraft, a bright planet or a satellite may all appear as similar pinpoints. Without knowing the actual distance, the observer cannot calculate true size or speed because those depend directly on range.

This becomes especially important in UFO reports. A witness may conclude that a light crossed "miles of sky in seconds", but the observation often measures only its angular movement across the observer's field of view. If the object's distance is unknown, its true velocity cannot be determined reliably. NASA's UAP assessment and aviation guidance both stress that missing range information is one of the biggest obstacles to interpreting aerial observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. ## Glare and brightness can make lights seem larger or nearer

Brightness is a poor guide to physical size.

Human vision tends to interpret intense lights as larger than their actual angular size because of glare and light scattering within the eye. Atmospheric moisture, haze and thin cloud can further spread light into a glowing halo that appears substantially bigger than the light source itself.

This can create several misleading impressions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A small but bright light may appear to be a much larger object.
* A glowing halo can make the source appear closer than it really is.
* Changes in atmospheric clarity may make the light seem to expand or shrink even if its actual size is unchanged.
* Brightness fluctuations caused by haze or thin cloud can resemble changes in distance or speed.

</div>

Modern aviation guidance warns that glare, reduced contrast and featureless night scenes routinely distort visual judgement, even among trained pilots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim/aim0801.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. ## Why stationary lights sometimes appear to move

One of the best documented night-time visual illusions is **autokinesis**.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://skybrary.aero/index.php/articles/visual-illusions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skybrary.aero">[skybrary.aero]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skybrary.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

When someone stares at a single stationary light against a dark background for several seconds, tiny involuntary eye movements occur continuously. Because there are no surrounding reference points, the brain may incorrectly interpret these eye movements as motion of the light itself.

The result can be the convincing impression that a star, planet or distant aircraft is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* drifting sideways;
* making small circles;
* suddenly changing direction;
* zig-zagging; or
* slowly approaching or receding.

</div>

The illusion has been recognised in aviation medicine for decades because pilots can mistakenly believe that a fixed light is moving. Flight training specifically teaches pilots to avoid staring continuously at isolated lights and instead use systematic scanning to reduce the effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. Medical Facts for Pilots</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. Medical Facts for Pilots</span></span></span>

## Clouds, haze and the disappearing horizon

Night conditions frequently remove the horizon that normally anchors visual perception.

When clouds obscure the skyline or haze blends the ground into the sky, observers lose an important reference for judging whether an object is climbing, descending or maintaining altitude.

Additional atmospheric effects further complicate interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Thin cloud can partially obscure a light, making it appear to blink or change shape.
* Passing haze alters brightness, creating the impression of acceleration or deceleration.
* Refraction through uneven layers of air near the horizon can slightly shift a light's apparent position.
* Scattered light around moisture or aerosols enlarges bright sources, making them appear closer than they are.

</div>

The Federal Aviation Administration identifies obscured horizons, dark scenes with sparse visual references and atmospheric conditions as well-known causes of visual illusion during night flight because they interfere with normal depth perception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. ## Why celebrity sightings are especially difficult to interpret

Within reports involving celebrities or other public figures, night-time observations often receive intense media attention despite having limited measurable information.

Many widely discussed accounts describe a bright, isolated light rather than a clearly resolved object. Because darkness removes distance cues, later descriptions may unintentionally exaggerate apparent speed, size or manoeuvrability. This does not imply that witnesses are dishonest. Instead, it reflects a fundamental limitation of human visual perception under low-light conditions.

Investigators therefore look for independent evidence such as multiple viewing locations, radar data, calibrated video, astronomical records or aircraft tracking before attempting to estimate an object's actual motion. Without such information, apparent extraordinary movement may remain impossible to distinguish from normal objects viewed under difficult night-time conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8. ## Why night observations require extra caution

Darkness does not merely make objects harder to see—it changes how the brain interprets what is seen. Losing depth cues, relying on brightness as a proxy for distance, experiencing glare and encountering illusions such as [autokinesis]({{ 'autokinesis/' | relative_url }}) all increase uncertainty when judging isolated lights.

For that reason, investigators treat witness estimates of distance, size and speed from night-time sightings with particular caution. Before concluding that a light performed extraordinary manoeuvres, the first question is usually the simplest: was there enough visual information to judge its distance in the first place? If the answer is no, then claims about remarkable motion become far less secure, regardless of how vivid or sincere the observation may have been.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8.
