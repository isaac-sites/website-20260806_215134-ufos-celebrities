## Introduction

Tom DeLonge did not prove that the US [Navy videos]({{ 'navy-videos/' | relative_url }}) showed extraterrestrial craft. His significance lies elsewhere: he helped transform a small collection of ambiguous military infrared recordings into cultural symbols of government "disclosure". By combining celebrity influence, entertainment production, former defence and intelligence officials, and carefully framed media appearances, DeLonge and [To the Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) (TTSA) presented the videos as evidence that official institutions were finally acknowledging a long-hidden mystery. The resulting narrative proved more influential than the technical content of the footage itself. While the US Department of Defense authenticated the videos and later released them officially, it consistently stated only that they depicted unidentified aerial phenomena—not alien technology or extraordinary craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

## Why DeLonge's celebrity platform mattered

Tom DeLonge entered the UFO field with something most researchers lacked: an established international audience. As the guitarist and vocalist of Blink-182, he had spent decades building media recognition, a loyal fan community and relationships with publishers, television producers and journalists. Rather than approaching the topic as an isolated enthusiast, he launched TTSA in 2017 as a multimedia company that deliberately combined entertainment, technology and public advocacy.

This mattered because celebrity changes how information travels. The Navy videos did not become globally recognised solely because they existed; they became cultural events because a well-known public figure repeatedly framed them as part of a larger story about official secrecy and imminent disclosure. Interviews, podcasts, television appearances and books allowed DeLonge to reach audiences who would never normally follow military aviation or UFO research. Contemporary reporting on TTSA emphasised this cross-[media strategy]({{ 'media-strategy/' | relative_url }}), describing an organisation intended to produce films, books, television and research alongside public outreach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. Equally important was the emotional framing. Rather than presenting isolated pieces of sensor footage, DeLonge consistently suggested that the videos represented fragments of a much larger reality that governments had begun acknowledging. This encouraged audiences to interpret each new official statement as another step in a continuing disclosure process rather than as routine clarification or bureaucratic transparency.

## The role of former defence and intelligence figures

TTSA's launch was unusual because DeLonge appeared alongside several former government officials whose careers gave institutional weight to the organisation's claims. Among the most prominent were Luis Elizondo, who said he had directed the Pentagon's Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), and former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense Christopher Mellon.

This combination created a persuasive communication model:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **DeLonge supplied public attention.** His celebrity attracted audiences and journalists.
* **Former officials supplied credibility.** Their backgrounds suggested access to government knowledge unavailable to ordinary observers.
* **The Navy videos supplied tangible evidence.** Unlike stories alone, the infrared recordings could be shown repeatedly on television and online.

</div>

The interaction between these elements mattered more than any single component. The footage by itself was technically complex and open to multiple interpretations. The former officials could describe unexplained military encounters, while DeLonge translated those discussions into a broader narrative that appealed to mainstream audiences.

When *The New York Times* published its influential December 2017 investigation into AATIP alongside two of the videos, TTSA simultaneously promoted the material through its own channels. The journalism, insider testimony and [celebrity advocacy]({{ 'official-actions/' | relative_url }}) reinforced one another, giving the impression of a coordinated moment of revelation rather than the gradual emergence of previously circulating footage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. ## How advocacy and entertainment reinforced one interpretation

TTSA was not structured as a traditional research institute. It combined commercial entertainment with advocacy, encouraging audiences to consume books, [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}), interviews and dramatic [storytelling]({{ 'storytelling/' | relative_url }}) alongside discussion of military incidents.

That approach had several effects.

First, it encouraged viewers to connect separate events into a single overarching narrative. The 2004 Nimitz encounter, the 2015 Roosevelt videos and later government statements became successive chapters in an unfolding disclosure story rather than distinct incidents with different evidential strengths.

Second, entertainment conventions influenced audience expectations. Television documentaries and promotional material naturally favour mystery, continuity and unanswered questions. This does not necessarily produce inaccurate reporting, but it can encourage audiences to interpret ambiguity as evidence that something extraordinary lies just beyond what is publicly known.

Third, repeated exposure normalised a particular interpretation. Viewers encountered the same infrared clips alongside interviews with former military personnel and intelligence officials, making the videos appear increasingly significant even though no new visual information had been added.

## Authentication versus disclosure

A central feature of the public narrative was the tendency to blur the distinction between three separate ideas:

1. The videos were genuine US Navy recordings.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/to-the-stars-academy-of-arts-science-tom-delonge-and-the-secret-dod-ufo-research-program/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[theblackvault.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
2. The objects in the videos had not been identified at the time.
3. Therefore, the government was disclosing evidence of extraordinary technology.

Only the first two propositions were officially supported.

In April 2020, the Department of Defense formally released the three videos after they had already circulated publicly for years. The Department explained that the purpose was to resolve public uncertainty about the [authenticity]({{ 'authenticity/' | relative_url }}) of the recordings while continuing to classify the observed phenomena simply as "unidentified". It did not state that the videos demonstrated alien spacecraft, breakthrough technology or any specific explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

Nevertheless, because the official release followed years of TTSA promotion, many observers interpreted the Pentagon's authentication as confirmation of the broader disclosure narrative that had already become culturally established.

## Why the Navy clips were so effective as symbols

The videos possessed characteristics that made them unusually powerful communication tools despite their limited evidential content.

Unlike eyewitness testimony, they originated from military sensor systems. Unlike classified documents, they could be shown repeatedly on television. Unlike lengthy technical reports, they lasted only seconds and could easily circulate on social media.

Their ambiguity also increased their usefulness. Because the recordings did not conclusively identify what they showed, they could support multiple narratives simultaneously. Supporters viewed them as evidence of extraordinary craft, while sceptics argued they reflected limitations of infrared imaging, targeting systems or ordinary aerial objects seen under unusual conditions.

Researchers including Mick West have proposed conventional explanations for apparent features in the Gimbal, GoFast and FLIR1 videos, such as infrared glare, camera rotation and parallax effects. Other analysts argue that these explanations do not fully account for the broader encounter context described by military witnesses. The continued disagreement illustrates that the videos themselves remain open to interpretation, even though their authenticity is not disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/threads/explained-the-navy-ufo-videos.11234/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[metabunk.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Explained: The Navy UFO Videos &#124; Metabunk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Explained: The Navy UFO Videos &#124; MetabunkApril 27, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 27, 2020</span></span></span>

## DeLonge's lasting influence on the UFO narrative

Tom DeLonge's most significant contribution was not discovering the Navy footage but changing how it was understood. He demonstrated that celebrity, institutional credibility and media production could reshape public interpretation of technically ambiguous evidence.

Before TTSA, the videos were obscure military recordings with limited public visibility. After TTSA's launch and the coordinated attention surrounding the 2017 reporting, they became widely recognised symbols of a perceived shift in official attitudes towards UFOs.

Whether one views that shift as overdue transparency, successful public advocacy or an example of persuasive framing, DeLonge helped establish the language of modern UFO discussion. The phrase "disclosure" became attached not because the Pentagon announced extraterrestrial visitors, but because celebrity promotion, former government insiders and carefully staged media narratives encouraged many audiences to interpret official acknowledgement of unidentified objects as evidence that a much larger truth was finally emerging.
