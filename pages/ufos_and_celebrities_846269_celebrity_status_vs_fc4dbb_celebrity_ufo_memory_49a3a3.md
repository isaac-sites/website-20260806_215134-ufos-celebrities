## Introduction

Celebrity UFO stories often become more detailed and more confidently told as the years pass, but psychological research shows that this does not necessarily make them more accurate. Human memory is reconstructive rather than a literal recording of past events. Each time a person recalls an experience—especially during interviews, [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}) or public discussions—they may strengthen some details, lose others or unintentionally incorporate later information into the memory. This matters because many well-known celebrity UFO accounts achieved widespread attention years or even decades after the reported sighting, after repeated retellings had already shaped the narrative. Understanding how memory changes over time helps explain why scientists treat the original contemporaneous account as stronger evidence than later, more elaborate recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

## The misinformation effect in eyewitness memory

One of the best-established findings in cognitive psychology is the **misinformation effect**: information acquired after an event can alter what a witness later remembers about that event. This does not require deliberate deception. Instead, later conversations, interview questions, books, news reports or documentaries can become blended with the original memory until the witness sincerely believes the revised account.

Research pioneered by Elizabeth Loftus and repeatedly replicated over several decades shows that even subtle suggestions—such as a leading question or an inaccurate narrative summary—can change later recall. Witnesses frequently express high confidence in these altered memories despite their reduced accuracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

## How interviews reward polished narratives

Public interviews rarely function like scientific witness interviews. Television, podcasts and magazine features naturally encourage memorable [storytelling]({{ 'storytelling/' | relative_url }}) rather than cautious uncertainty.

Repeated media appearances can reshape memories through several mechanisms.

**Narrative smoothing.** Early recollections often contain uncertainty, hesitation and incomplete descriptions. After multiple retellings, those gaps tend to disappear, producing a cleaner story that feels easier to follow.

**Selection of memorable details.** Interviewers often return to striking elements—unusual lights, sudden movements or emotional reactions. These become central to the story, while ordinary contextual details receive less attention and may gradually fade.

**Confirmation feedback.** When audiences react enthusiastically to particular parts of a story, those portions are more likely to be repeated in future interviews, reinforcing them in the witness's own memory.

**Repeated retrieval.** Every act of remembering reactivates the memory. Research on repeated recall shows that witnesses may remember additional details in later interviews, but repeated retrieval can also introduce new errors or strengthen incorrect information alongside genuine memories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24098471/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Are two interviews better than one? eyewitness memory across repeated cognitive interviews - PubMedOctober 3, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 3, 2013</span></span></span>

None of these mechanisms requires intentional embellishment. They arise from normal memory processes and from the incentives of public storytelling.

## Why celebrity UFO accounts often change over decades

Many celebrity sightings entered popular culture long after the reported event. This creates an unusually long period during which memories can be reshaped before becoming widely documented.

During those intervening years, a witness may:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* recount the experience privately many times;
* encounter changing public ideas about UFOs;
* read media coverage of similar cases;
* answer increasingly specific interview questions; or
* revisit earlier versions of their own story.

</div>

Because memory is reconstructive, these later influences can become difficult to distinguish from the original perception itself. Psychological research shows that people are often poor at identifying the true source of remembered information, confusing something they experienced directly with something they later heard or imagined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

This does not mean the original event never occurred. It means the precision of later details should not automatically be assumed to reflect the precision of the original observation.

## A useful contrast: contemporary versus retrospective accounts

A helpful illustration comes from [John Lennon]({{ 'john-lennon/' | relative_url }})'s 1974 UFO report. One reason researchers continue to discuss the case is that Lennon publicly referred to the sighting close to the time it allegedly occurred, including a brief note on the *Walls and Bridges* album stating that he had seen a UFO on 23 August 1974.

That contemporaneous reference is generally considered more valuable than much later retellings because it establishes that Lennon was making the claim immediately after the event rather than creating the story decades later. Later interviews may provide richer descriptions, but those additional details also have had much more opportunity to be influenced by repeated recall and subsequent discussion. The difference is not that [early accounts]({{ 'early-accounts/' | relative_url }}) are guaranteed to be correct; rather, they have undergone fewer opportunities for memory drift.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

## Why early independent accounts deserve more weight

For investigators comparing competing explanations, the timing of a statement matters almost as much as its content.

Early accounts generally have several advantages:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* they are closer in time to the original perception;
* they contain fewer opportunities for post-event contamination;
* they pre-date most public discussion of the case;
* they are less likely to have incorporated details from media coverage; and
* they preserve uncertainty that may later disappear through storytelling.

</div>

[Independent accounts]({{ 'independent-accounts/' | relative_url }}) from multiple witnesses recorded before they have discussed the event with one another are especially valuable. If those accounts agree on important observations despite being collected separately, they provide stronger evidence than memories that converge only after years of shared discussion. This principle is widely used in eyewitness research as well as scientific investigations of unusual aerial phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

## What this means when evaluating celebrity UFO stories

Understanding memory drift does not require assuming that celebrity witnesses are dishonest or mistaken about everything they experienced. A person may genuinely remember seeing something unusual while simultaneously remembering some surrounding details less accurately than they originally occurred.

For that reason, scientific investigations separate two questions:

1. **Did the witness sincerely report an unusual experience?**
2. **How accurately does the present-day account preserve the original observation?**

Media attention can substantially increase confidence, detail and cultural influence without increasing evidential reliability. Consequently, later [celebrity interviews]({{ 'media-gateway/' | relative_url }}) are most useful when interpreted alongside the earliest available records, independent witness statements and any objective evidence such as photographs, radar data or contemporaneous documentation. Psychological research consistently indicates that the passage of time and repeated public retelling make memories more polished, but not necessarily more historically accurate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>
