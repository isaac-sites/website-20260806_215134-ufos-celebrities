## Introduction

Authentic UFO footage can prove that a recording is genuine, that it came from a military sensor and that investigators have not yet identified what appears in it. It does **not** prove that the object is extraterrestrial. Authenticity answers a narrow question — “Is this the real recording?” — while identification requires much more: reliable distance, altitude, speed, direction, sensor settings, environmental conditions and comparison with ordinary aircraft, balloons, birds and optical effects.

A clip can be unaltered and intriguing while remaining scientifically inconclusive. When an authority confirms that footage is authentic, it is normally confirming the file’s provenance rather than endorsing every claim made about its contents. The announcement also said their release would not expose sensitive systems and that the objects shown remained unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.secnav.navy.mil/foia/readingroom/CaseFiles/UAP%20INFO/UAP%20DOCUMENTS/PAO%20Briefing%20Card%202020-012022%20and%202022-006563.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: secnav.navy.mil">[Secretary of the Navy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">secnav.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PAO Briefing Card 2020 012022 and 2022 006563</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The program generated significant interest in limited-reach outlets that focus on “UFO” theories and conspiracies. ## Missing distance changes everything

Video provides angular information: an object appears at a certain point in the frame and shifts by a certain amount. It does not necessarily reveal the object’s true size, distance or ground speed. Without those quantities, dramatic-looking motion can be profoundly misleading.

A small nearby object and a large distant object may occupy the same number of pixels. Likewise, an object crossing the frame quickly may be moving rapidly, or it may only appear to do so because the camera platform is moving, turning or zooming. NASA’s independent UAP study stressed that distance is essential for evaluating claims of extreme speed or acceleration. Without a reliable range measurement, apparent performance cannot be converted confidently into physical performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6a4fc54602e71" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA should leverage its core capabilities and expertise to determine whether it should take a leading or supporting role in implementing...</span></span></span>

The 2015 *GoFast* recording offers a concrete example. The clip appears to show a small object racing just above the sea. AARO’s later reconstruction placed it at roughly 13,000 feet rather than close to the water. Its analysis found no anomalous performance and concluded that the impression of extraordinary speed was caused by motion parallax — the apparent displacement produced when a moving observer views another object against a more distant background. Depending on assumptions about heading and wind, AARO estimated a wind-adjusted speed between approximately 5 and 92 miles per hour, not the extraordinary velocity suggested by a casual viewing. The exact identity remained uncertain, but the supposed high-speed capability did not survive geometric analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Defense Case: “Go Fast” Case Resolution &#124; February 6, 2025 Case Overview In January 2015, a U.S. Navy F/A-18F pil...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 6, 2025</span></span></span>

## Cameras record sensor behaviour as well as objects

Military infrared footage is often treated as if it were a direct window onto the scene. In reality, the image is produced by a chain of hardware and software: lenses, detectors, tracking systems, stabilisation, contrast processing, zoom, compression and display settings. What appears on screen is a representation of infrared intensity shaped by that chain.

This matters because a point-like object can look larger, smoother or stranger than its physical outline. Compression may create trails, halos or block-like structures. Automatic tracking can keep an object centred while the background moves, obscuring which part of the apparent motion comes from the aircraft, the sensor turret or the target. Changes in zoom and contrast can also make an object seem to expand, contract or alter form.

NASA’s study found that UAP recordings are frequently collected by sensors that were not designed or calibrated for scientific investigation of anomalous objects. Associated metadata — including time, location, observing mode and calibration information — may be incomplete. Without that context, analysts have greater difficulty distinguishing a physical feature from a sensor artefact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=69d735b96d21b" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">uap independent study team final report 0</span></span></span>

One military recording from South Asia appeared to show an object with an unusual atmospheric wake. After analysts examined longer-focal-length footage and compared the event with commercial flight data, AARO assessed that the object was probably an ordinary aircraft and that the apparent wake was an artefact produced by video compression. A visually striking feature in authentic footage was therefore not necessarily a feature of the object at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=69fb208d27af8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">uap independent study team final report 0</span></span></span>

Authenticity cannot solve this problem. A sensor artefact contained in an original recording is still authentically recorded.

## Apparent manoeuvres may belong to the viewpoint

Viewers naturally interpret a video as though the camera were stationary. A military aircraft, however, may be travelling hundreds of miles per hour while banking, changing altitude and rotating a gimballed sensor. The resulting image combines several movements:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the object’s actual motion;
* the recording aircraft’s motion;
* the camera’s rotation and stabilisation;
* the changing line of sight;
* alterations in zoom or field of view;
* background movement caused by parallax.

</div>

Separating these components requires telemetry and reconstruction, not merely watching the clip repeatedly.

The [Puerto Rico]({{ 'puerto-rico/' | relative_url }}) infrared video recorded near [Aguadilla]({{ 'aguadilla/' | relative_url }}) in 2013 demonstrates how radically interpretation can change. The footage appeared to show a fast object crossing the landscape, dividing into two and entering or emerging from the sea. AARO’s reconstruction concluded with high confidence that the objects did not display anomalous speed or transmedium movement. It assessed that two objects travelled in a straight line at approximately wind speed and were intermittently obscured by clouds; it judged them, with moderate confidence, to be sky lanterns. The aircraft’s curved flight path, sensor geometry and changing range contributed to the appearance of rapid and unusual movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_Puerto_Rico_UAP_Case_Resolution.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Puerto Rico UAP Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Puerto Rico UAP Case Resolution</span></span></span>

The case illustrates why a sequence that looks self-explanatory may not be. Human vision is good at following shapes but poor at recovering three-dimensional geometry from a single moving viewpoint without known reference distances.

## “Physical object” still does not mean exotic craft

Another common leap occurs when investigators say that footage probably depicts a real physical object. That finding may rule out a purely electronic anomaly, but it leaves a wide range of ordinary possibilities.

AARO’s public imagery includes cases in which analysts judged with high confidence that a physical object was present while finding its visible shape and behaviour unremarkable. Some remained unattributed because the recording lacked enough information for a specific identification. Others were assessed as balloons or migratory birds after their motion, morphology and infrared behaviour were compared with known examples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/Middle-East-Object/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Imagery</span></span></span>

This creates a hierarchy of claims that is often collapsed in popular discussion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **The file is genuine.**
2. **The sensor detected a real signal.**
3. **The signal probably came from a physical object.**
4. **The object has not been specifically identified.**
5. **The object demonstrated genuinely anomalous performance.**
6. **The object was manufactured by a non-human civilisation.**

</div>

Each step requires additional evidence. Confirmation of the first or third does not automatically support the fifth, much less the sixth.

Recent official reporting reinforces this point. AARO has resolved numerous cases as balloons, birds, drones, satellites and aircraft, while other reports remain open because the data are inadequate. Its fiscal-year reporting states that it has found no evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity or technology. [Unresolved cases]({{ 'unresolved-cases/' | relative_url }}) are therefore not counted as alien evidence; they are cases in which attribution has not yet met the required confidence threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.defense.gov/2024/Nov/14/2003583603/-1/-1/0/FY25-CONSOLIDATED-ANNUAL-REPORT-ON-UAP-508.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 CONSOLIDATED ANNUAL REPORT ON UAP 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FY25 CONSOLIDATED ANNUAL REPORT ON UAP 508</span></span></span>

## How ambiguous clips acquire alien certainty

The strongest alien interpretation is often added after the recording leaves its original operational setting. A short clip may circulate without the full sensor sequence, telemetry, flight path, weather data or analyst commentary. A broadcaster then pairs it with excited pilot dialogue, dramatic music or claims about impossible speed. Celebrity advocates may repeat the most extraordinary reading to large audiences, while technical qualifications receive less attention.

Three habits make overreading especially easy.

**Treating official release as official endorsement.** Government publication can look like validation of an extraordinary explanation. In reality, a defence organisation may release footage for transparency, flight-safety analysis or clarification of provenance while expressly withholding judgement about identity.

**Assuming the most dramatic visual interpretation is the only one.** An object that seems to skim the ocean may be thousands of feet above it. A shape that appears to rotate may reflect camera optics or changing infrared glare. A trail may originate in compression rather than the atmosphere.

**Using lack of identification as positive evidence.** When records are incomplete, “unresolved” often means that no explanation can be established with sufficient confidence. It does not mean investigators have eliminated every conventional possibility. The 2021 US intelligence assessment emphasised that inconsistent reporting and limited data prevented detailed analysis of many events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.secnav.navy.mil/foia/readingroom/CaseFiles/UAP%20INFO/UAP%20DOCUMENTS/UAP%20Prelimiary%20Assessment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: secnav.navy.mil">[Secretary of the Navy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">secnav.navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Prelimiary Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Prelimiary Assessment</span></span></span>

These errors do not require fraud or bad faith. Pilots can report sincerely, military recordings can be genuine and public figures can honestly find them compelling. The evidential problem arises when uncertainty is converted into certainty without the missing measurements.

## What footage would need to show

A video could contribute to a serious case for genuinely anomalous technology, but the evidential standard would be far higher than authenticity alone. Investigators would need enough independent information to reconstruct the event and test ordinary explanations.

Useful evidence would include calibrated observations from more than one location, reliable range measurements, complete sensor metadata, aircraft telemetry, radar tracks, weather and wind information, unedited footage before and after the event, and records from independent systems. Multiple viewpoints could permit [triangulation]({{ 'triangulation/' | relative_url }}), turning apparent angular motion into measurable distance, velocity and acceleration. Spectral information might help determine composition or temperature, while repeat observations under controlled conditions would reduce the chance of a one-off artefact.

NASA’s independent study recommended better-calibrated instruments, consistent metadata, multisensor collection and systematic comparison with known events. Its central point was not that unusual footage should be ignored, but that useful scientific conclusions require measurements designed to distinguish the abnormal from the merely unfamiliar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=69d735b96d21b" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">uap independent study team final report 0</span></span></span>

Authentic footage is therefore a starting point. It can establish that an event deserves analysis and may preserve clues that later prove valuable. But until distance, motion, sensor behaviour and alternative explanations are constrained, a genuine military video remains evidence of an observation — not proof of aliens.

    });

      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;

    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
