## Introduction

Public flight-tracking websites are valuable tools when assessing a reported UFO or celebrity UFO sighting, but they are not definitive records of everything that was in the sky. A match between a reported object and a recorded aircraft can often resolve a sighting quickly. However, the absence of a flight on a public tracker does **not** prove that no aircraft was present. Public websites depend on specific data sources, receiver networks, legal restrictions and commercial policies, all of which create blind spots.

For anyone evaluating a UFO report, flight records should therefore be treated as one piece of evidence rather than a final verdict. The strongest investigations compare multiple aviation datasets, understand the limitations of each source and distinguish between "no matching aircraft was found" and "no aircraft existed."

## Which aviation records can reconstruct a sighting?

A useful reconstruction begins by identifying every aircraft that could plausibly have occupied the reported location and time. Public tracking sites provide an accessible starting point, but investigators should understand what information they actually display.

The most useful aviation records include:

* **ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast)** data, in which equipped aircraft broadcast their identity, position, altitude and speed.
* **Mode S transponder** messages, which can reveal aircraft presence even when complete position information is unavailable.
* **Air traffic control records**, radar data and surveillance logs, where available through official investigations or freedom-of-information requests.
* **Historical flight databases**, which preserve many commercial flight tracks after the event.
* **Airport movement records**, published schedules and departure logs that can confirm aircraft operations even if complete tracks are unavailable.

No single dataset covers every aircraft. Civil aviation relies on several surveillance systems operating together, and public websites usually expose only part of that information.

This distinction is particularly relevant when celebrity UFO stories emerge. If a famous witness reports unusual lights near an airport or along a busy airway, comparing the observation with aviation records is an essential first step. However, investigators should avoid treating one website's search results as the complete picture.

## Why public tracking websites have blind spots

Flight-tracking services differ because they collect information from different sources and operate under different legal and commercial constraints.

### Coverage depends on receivers

Many tracking websites rely heavily on networks of volunteer ground receivers that collect ADS-B transmissions. Coverage is generally excellent over populated regions but weaker over oceans, mountains, deserts and sparsely populated areas. Aircraft may temporarily disappear from public maps simply because no receiver is available to hear their transmissions. Research using the OpenSky Network has shown that terrestrial ADS-B coverage leaves significant gaps over remote regions, leading researchers to combine ADS-B with satellite-based ADS-C data to improve trajectory reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OpenSky Report 2025: Improving Crowdsourced Flight Trajectories with ADS-C DataMay 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2025</span></span></span>

Temporary reception failures can also occur because of antenna placement, terrain shielding, equipment outages or radio interference.

### Not every aircraft broadcasts the same data

Different aircraft carry different surveillance equipment, and not every flight continuously broadcasts the information that public websites display.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Older aircraft using limited transponder capabilities.
* Military aircraft with restricted or altered transmissions.
* Government and law-enforcement flights.
* Aircraft operating under approved privacy programmes.
* Certain helicopters and specialist operations at low altitude where reception is poor.

</div>

Consequently, a public map should never be interpreted as a complete inventory of all aircraft in the sky.

### Privacy programmes intentionally reduce visibility

In the United States, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) operates the **Limiting Aircraft Data Displayed (LADD)** programme, allowing eligible operators to request that identifying flight information be withheld from participating public tracking services. The FAA also operates the **Privacy ICAO Address (PIA)** programme, which assigns temporary ICAO aircraft addresses to improve privacy for eligible aircraft meeting specific operational requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/pilots/ladd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation AdministrationLimiting Aircraft Data Displayed (LADD) &#124; Federal Aviation AdministrationMay 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2025</span></span></span>

These programmes exist for privacy and security reasons rather than to conceal unusual activity. Business aircraft, charter operators and some high-profile individuals may legitimately use them.

Flight-tracking services themselves acknowledge that some flights are blocked, anonymised or omitted because of these programmes or because certain government aircraft are intentionally excluded from public display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://support.fr24.com/support/solutions/articles/3000117426-why-is-some-aircraft-information-limited-or-not-visible-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: support.fr24.com">[Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">support.fr24.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Why is some aircraft information limited or not visible?: Flightradar24 Support centerApril 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2026</span></span></span>

### Different websites show different aircraft

Another common misunderstanding is that all tracking sites display identical information. They do not.

Some services primarily redistribute FAA or other official feeds and therefore comply with programmes such as LADD. Others rely more heavily on independently collected ADS-B broadcasts and may display aircraft differently, although they remain subject to the technical limits of their own receiver networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://support.adsbexchange.com/hc/en-us/articles/36737581489037-How-Can-My-Aircraft-Be-Removed-From-the-ADS-B-Exchange-Website" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: support.adsbexchange.com">[adsbexchange.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">support.adsbexchange.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ADS-B Exchange Help CenterHow Can My Aircraft Be Removed From the ADS-B Exchange Website? – ADS-B Exchange Help CenterJanuary 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2026</span></span></span>

As a result, comparing multiple independent datasets is often more informative than relying on a single website.

## How to compare a reported path with recorded traffic

Flight data becomes most useful when compared systematically with the witness account rather than searched casually.

A practical approach is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Establish an accurate time.** Even a difference of two or three minutes can change which aircraft were nearby.
2. **Fix the observer's location.** Aircraft geometry changes dramatically over relatively short distances.
3. **Determine the viewing direction and elevation.** This allows comparison with reconstructed flight paths rather than simple map proximity.
4. **Check multiple flight databases.** Differences between services may reveal missing tracks or privacy filtering.
5. **Compare altitude, heading and lighting.** Landing lights, anti-collision strobes and navigation lights often explain apparently unusual behaviour.
6. **Consider viewing geometry.** An aircraft flying towards an observer may appear almost stationary before seeming to accelerate sideways, creating an illusion of unusual motion.

</div>

When possible, investigators should compare recorded flight paths against the witness's line of sight rather than asking only whether an aircraft was "nearby."

## When missing flight data should not be overinterpreted

One of the most common reasoning errors in UFO investigations is treating the absence of public flight data as positive evidence for an extraordinary explanation.

A missing public track can result from many ordinary factors:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* incomplete ADS-B reception;
* temporary network outages;
* privacy filtering;
* military or government restrictions;
* equipment differences;
* historical archive limitations; or
* data-processing choices made by individual tracking providers.

</div>

These possibilities mean that "no aircraft found" is not equivalent to "no aircraft existed."

The opposite mistake is equally important to avoid. A recorded aircraft passing through the area does not automatically explain every sighting. Investigators must still compare timing, direction, altitude, apparent brightness and witness descriptions to determine whether the recorded flight plausibly matches what was observed.

## What flight records can—and cannot—tell us

Flight records are among the strongest forms of contextual evidence available when assessing UFO reports because they provide independently recorded, time-stamped information that can often identify ordinary aircraft. They are particularly valuable for reconstructing celebrity sightings that receive intense public attention, since they allow claims to be checked against objective aviation data rather than relying solely on memory or media reports.

Their limitation is equally important. Public tracking websites are observational tools built from incomplete datasets, not comprehensive surveillance systems. A convincing investigation therefore combines flight records with precise witness timing, viewing geometry, weather conditions, astronomical checks and original photographic evidence. Used in that broader context, aviation records can eliminate many conventional explanations while avoiding the mistaken assumption that an unlisted aircraft could not have been present.
