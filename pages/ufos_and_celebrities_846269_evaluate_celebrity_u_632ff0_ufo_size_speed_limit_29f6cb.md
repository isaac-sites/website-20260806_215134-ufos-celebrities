## Introduction

When evaluating a celebrity UFO claim, one of the easiest mistakes is to treat estimates of an object's size, speed or acceleration as measured facts. In most cases they are not. Unless the witness knows how far away the object was, they cannot reliably calculate its true dimensions or velocity. A small nearby object and a much larger distant object can produce the same apparent size in the sky, while an ordinary aircraft at long range can appear to make abrupt turns or extreme accelerations because of perspective. NASA's independent UAP study highlights that eyewitness reports often lack the calibrated measurements needed to determine an object's true behaviour, making visual estimates alone an unreliable basis for extraordinary conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

For celebrity sightings, this principle is especially important. Public attention often focuses on dramatic descriptions such as "the object was enormous" or "it crossed the sky instantly". Those descriptions may accurately reflect what the witness perceived, but perception is not the same as a physical measurement.

## Could the Witness Really Judge Its Speed?

### Angular size versus true size

The human eye measures only the angle an object occupies in the field of view, not its physical size. A bright point of light offers almost no information about distance by itself.

* A drone 100 metres away.

* A bright planet near the horizon.
* An aircraft's landing lights tens of kilometres away.

Each can appear as a similarly sized point of light despite enormous differences in actual size and distance.

Vision researchers have long shown that perceived size depends heavily on available depth cues rather than on retinal image alone. When those cues disappear—as they often do against a dark or empty sky—people become much less accurate at judging absolute distance and therefore true size.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/1960s/1962/196215" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

This is why investigators avoid treating statements such as "it was the size of a football stadium" as literal measurements unless the witness can independently establish how far away the object was.

### Why distance controls speed estimates

Speed cannot be calculated from appearance alone.

The relationship is simple:

**True speed = angular movement × distance**

If the distance is unknown, every speed estimate becomes speculative.

An object that moves ten degrees across the sky in five seconds could be:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a nearby bird moving relatively slowly;
* a drone at moderate speed;
* an aircraft much farther away;
* or, if genuinely distant, something travelling much faster.

</div>

Without knowing which distance is correct, all four possibilities remain mathematically consistent with the same apparent motion.

The same problem affects claims of extreme acceleration. If a nearby object changes direction slightly, perspective can exaggerate that motion dramatically. Conversely, a distant aircraft flying steadily towards or away from the observer may appear almost stationary before seeming to move suddenly across the sky.

NASA's UAP report repeatedly emphasises that reliable conclusions require independent measurements—such as radar, multiple synchronised cameras or precisely calibrated sensors—rather than visual impressions alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why an Empty Sky Is So Misleading

Human vision estimates distance using cues such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* foreground objects;
* overlapping objects (occlusion);
* shadows;
* binocular depth;
* familiar object sizes;
* texture and perspective.

</div>

Most of these cues disappear when someone looks into an open sky, especially at night.

A single bright light against darkness provides almost no direct information about range. The brain therefore fills in missing information using assumptions, which can vary considerably between observers.

This limitation affects everyone, including experienced observers. Aviation research has documented that judging distance and relative position in sparse visual environments is inherently difficult because absolute depth cues become weak or unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/1960s/1962/196215" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

That does not mean the witness imagined the object. It means that estimates of its physical properties deserve much less confidence than the observation that "a light was present and appeared to move."

## Using Landmarks and Occlusion as Constraints

Investigators gain much stronger evidence when a reported object interacts with known features of the environment.

Useful constraints include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* passing behind a building or mountain;
* disappearing behind clouds whose approximate altitude is known;
* crossing in front of trees or power lines;
* moving relative to identifiable stars;
* being recorded simultaneously from multiple locations.

</div>

These observations create geometric limits on where the object could have been.

For example, if an object disappears behind a nearby hill, it cannot have been beyond that hill from the observer's viewpoint. Likewise, if multiple cameras from different locations record the same object simultaneously, [triangulation]({{ 'triangulation/' | relative_url }}) may estimate its distance far more accurately than a single eyewitness ever could.

Without such reference points, distance remains largely unconstrained, and estimates of size or speed remain correspondingly uncertain.

## Why Perspective Creates Illusions of Extreme Motion

Perspective can make ordinary movement appear extraordinary.

An object moving mostly towards the observer produces very little sideways motion until its viewing angle changes, after which it may seem to dart across the sky. Likewise, a nearby insect crossing the observer's line of sight can appear to move much faster than a distant aircraft because it sweeps through a larger visual angle in less time.

Rapid apparent changes in brightness can also reinforce misleading impressions. Aircraft navigation lights, rotating beacons, atmospheric haze and changing viewing angles may cause an object to appear to accelerate, stop or vanish suddenly without any unusual physical manoeuvre.

These effects explain why visual impressions alone are poor evidence for estimating extraordinary flight performance.

## What This Means When Assessing a Celebrity UFO Claim

[Celebrity status]({{ 'fame-vs-evidence/' | relative_url }}) does not alter the geometry of visual perception. An actor, musician or astronaut may sincerely report that an object looked immense or impossibly fast, but those impressions should be separated from measurable facts.

When assessing such claims, ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the distance independently established?
* Were there landmarks that constrained the object's position?
* Did multiple observers view it from different locations?
* Were radar, photographs or calibrated instruments available?
* Could the reported speed simply reflect angular motion without a known range?

</div>

If the answer to the first questions is no, then estimates of size, speed and acceleration should be treated as informed impressions rather than physical measurements.
