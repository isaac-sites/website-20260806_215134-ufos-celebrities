## Introduction

The three videos commonly called **FLIR1**, **Gimbal** and **GoFast** did not suddenly appear when the Pentagon published them in April 2020. They had already been circulating for years through a combination of online leaks, investigative journalism and promotion by **[To the Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }})**, the organisation co-founded by musician Tom DeLonge. The Pentagon's action was therefore not the first public release of the footage. Instead, it was an official authentication and authorised publication of material that had already entered public circulation through different routes. This distinction matters because much of the later public debate confused *official confirmation that the videos were genuine Navy recordings* with *official endorsement of extraordinary interpretations*. The Department of Defense was careful to make no such endorsement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

The oldest clip, **FLIR1**, was associated with the USS *Nimitz* encounter of November 2004. Versions of the footage appeared online as early as 2007 through unofficial channels. Years later, the Department of Defense itself acknowledged that the videos had been circulating after "unauthorised releases in 2007 and 2017", confirming that public access began well before the Pentagon's own publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

The remaining videos entered widespread public awareness much later. In December 2017, *The New York Times* published a major investigation into the Pentagon's Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), embedding the **FLIR1** and **Gimbal** videos alongside reporting on the programme. At roughly the same time, To the Stars Academy published the footage through its own channels, with former defence officials including Luis Elizondo and Christopher Mellon helping explain the recordings to journalists and television audiences. **GoFast** followed shortly afterwards and received broad attention during 2018.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/navy-confirms-ufo-videos-real" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy Confirms UFO Videos Are Real and Show Unidentified Aerial Phenomena &#124; HISTORY...</span></span></span>

The result was that millions of people encountered the videos through news organisations and a private advocacy group rather than through an official Pentagon press release.

## FLIR1's Early Online Circulation

The 2007 appearance of FLIR1 remains one of the most unusual parts of the story because the video's path into the public domain was never presented as a formal government publication.

Copies circulated on the internet within UFO communities years before the footage became internationally famous. At the time, the clip attracted limited mainstream attention because there was no official confirmation of its origin, little accompanying documentation and no coordinated media campaign explaining its significance. Without authoritative context, many viewers regarded it as simply another disputed internet video.

Only after later reporting connected the recording to the 2004 USS *Nimitz* incident did the earlier leak become historically important. The early circulation demonstrated that genuine military material could appear online long before the government acknowledged it publicly, but the leak alone did not persuade most mainstream observers because its provenance remained uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

## The 2017 Journalism and To the Stars Releases

The decisive change came in late 2017, not because new footage appeared, but because established institutions supplied context and credibility.

*The New York Times* linked the videos to reporting on AATIP, describing a previously little-known Pentagon programme that investigated reports of unidentified aerial phenomena. The accompanying article included interviews with former officials and military personnel, transforming obscure infrared clips into evidence associated with a documented government project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">what is up with those pentagon ufo videos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. At nearly the same time, To the Stars Academy amplified the material through a different route. The organisation combined former government officials, media production and Tom DeLonge's celebrity profile. It argued that the videos had passed through official review processes before release, although later reporting noted continuing questions about the precise administrative approvals involved and whether clearance for public release was identical to formal Department of Defense publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/does-it-matter-that-the-dod-released-those-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Does It Matter That the DOD Released Those UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These videos, previously surfaced through The New York Times in 2017, were not initially authorized by the Pentagon, though they were ack...</span></span></span>

Rather than a single official announcement, public awareness grew through overlapping media channels that reinforced one another.

## Why the Pentagon Published the Videos in 2020

By April 2020 the recordings were already widely available, so the Pentagon's decision addressed a different problem.

The Department of Defense stated that it was authorising release because the videos had already circulated publicly after unauthorised disclosures. Following review, officials concluded that publishing the unclassified recordings would not reveal sensitive capabilities or interfere with investigations. The department also said it wanted to eliminate public confusion over whether the circulating footage was authentic or whether additional hidden footage existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

That statement accomplished three specific things:

* it confirmed the videos were genuine Navy recordings;
* it established that their official release would not compromise national security;
* it reiterated that the observed aerial phenomena remained **unidentified**, without offering an explanation or endorsing extraterrestrial interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

This is an important distinction. The Pentagon authenticated the source of the footage, not the extraordinary claims sometimes attached to it.

## Why the Official Release Changed the Story

Although the clips themselves were largely familiar by 2020, official publication fundamentally changed their status.

Before April 2020, debates often centred on whether the recordings were genuine military videos or fabricated material. After the Pentagon's release, that question largely disappeared because the Department of Defense had formally accepted ownership of the footage. Attention shifted instead to what, if anything, the videos actually showed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

The official release also demonstrated how separate stages of disclosure can shape public understanding:

* **Unofficial circulation (2007):** genuine material reached the public without authoritative confirmation.
* **Journalistic investigation (2017):** the videos gained national attention by being linked to reporting on a real Pentagon programme.
* **To the Stars promotion (2017–2018):** celebrity influence and former defence officials expanded the audience dramatically.
* **Pentagon authorisation (2020):** the government authenticated the recordings while explicitly leaving the objects classified only as unidentified aerial phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[defense.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>

Understanding this release trail helps explain why the phrase "Pentagon UFO videos" can be misleading. The Pentagon did not first introduce the recordings to the public in 2020; it formally validated and republished footage that had already travelled through leaks, investigative reporting and a high-profile public campaign years earlier.
