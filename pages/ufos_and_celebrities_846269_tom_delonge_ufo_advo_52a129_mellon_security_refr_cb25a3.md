## Introduction

Christopher Mellon helped change the public conversation about UFOs by arguing that the central issue was not whether unidentified objects were extraterrestrial, but whether the United States was adequately protecting its military airspace and understanding unexplained incursions. As a former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and former staff director of the Senate Intelligence Committee, Mellon brought institutional credibility to a debate long associated with fringe speculation. Within Tom DeLonge's advocacy network, his role was distinct: rather than promoting theories about alien visitors, he consistently presented unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) as a governance, intelligence and aviation-safety problem requiring professional investigation. That narrower framing proved influential because it gave military officials, journalists and legislators a way to discuss unexplained incidents without first accepting extraordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/the-military-keeps-encountering-ufos-why-doesnt-the-pentagon-care/2018/03/09/242c125c-22ee-11e8-94da-ebf9d112159c_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostThe military keeps encountering UFOs. ## Mellon's airspace and intelligence argument

Mellon's core claim was straightforward: unidentified objects repeatedly reported near military training areas represented an intelligence gap regardless of their ultimate origin. If pilots, radar operators and other military personnel were observing objects that could not immediately be identified, then defence organisations had an obligation to determine whether they were foreign surveillance systems, advanced drones, sensor anomalies or something else entirely.

Rather than arguing that the released [Navy videos]({{ 'navy-videos/' | relative_url }}) proved alien spacecraft, Mellon criticised the absence of a coordinated Pentagon process for collecting, analysing and sharing reports across military services. He argued that the United States could not assess potential threats because it was not treating the reports as an integrated intelligence problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/the-military-keeps-encountering-ufos-why-doesnt-the-pentagon-care/2018/03/09/242c125c-22ee-11e8-94da-ebf9d112159c_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostThe military keeps encountering UFOs. Several features distinguished this argument:

* **Unknown did not mean extraterrestrial.** Mellon repeatedly emphasised that "unidentified" described the current state of knowledge, not the object's origin.
* **Military readiness came first.** Reports from restricted training ranges raised concerns about surveillance, operational security and flight safety regardless of whether the explanation proved mundane or extraordinary.
* **Better data mattered more than stronger beliefs.** His proposals focused on reporting systems, sensor fusion, intelligence sharing and oversight rather than on proving sensational claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/the-military-keeps-encountering-ufos-why-doesnt-the-pentagon-care/2018/03/09/242c125c-22ee-11e8-94da-ebf9d112159c_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostThe military keeps encountering UFOs. This emphasis fit naturally with his background in defence policy. Instead of asking, "Are UFOs real?", Mellon encouraged policymakers to ask whether the military possessed adequate procedures for understanding repeated incursions into sensitive airspace.

## Why the narrower frame reduced stigma

For decades, discussion of UFOs often became trapped between two extremes: outright dismissal or confident claims of alien visitation. Mellon's security framing created a third position that many officials considered professionally acceptable.

By focusing on military reporting failures, he shifted attention from belief to process. Pilots who reported unusual encounters no longer needed to claim they had seen extraterrestrial craft. They merely needed to report observations that deserved technical analysis.

This approach reduced several longstanding barriers:

* **Career risk.** Military personnel could discuss unusual encounters as aviation safety incidents rather than paranormal experiences.
* **Political credibility.** Legislators could request investigations without endorsing extraordinary explanations.
* **Media treatment.** News organisations increasingly covered UAP alongside defence, intelligence and aviation reporting instead of relegating the subject to entertainment or conspiracy coverage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/national-security/head-of-pentagons-secret-ufo-office-sought-to-make-evidence-public/2017/12/16/90bcb7cc-e2b2-11e7-8679-a9728984779c_story.html?itid=lk_inline_manual_9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostHead of Pentagon’s secret ‘UFO’ office sought to make evidence public - The Washington Post...</span></span></span>

Within Tom DeLonge's wider network, this distinction was important. DeLonge himself frequently spoke about broader theories concerning non-human intelligence, whereas Mellon generally confined his public advocacy to questions of institutional competence, intelligence oversight and national security. That division of roles allowed the network to appeal simultaneously to enthusiasts and to policymakers, although critics also argued that the association with more speculative figures complicated Mellon's effort to maintain a narrower focus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/national-security/head-of-pentagons-secret-ufo-office-sought-to-make-evidence-public/2017/12/16/90bcb7cc-e2b2-11e7-8679-a9728984779c_story.html?itid=lk_inline_manual_9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostHead of Pentagon’s secret ‘UFO’ office sought to make evidence public - The Washington Post...</span></span></span>

## The gap between investigation and extraordinary claims

A defining feature of Mellon's public position was his insistence that investigation should not be confused with proof.

He argued that unexplained military encounters justified systematic inquiry because several ordinary possibilities carried serious implications:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* advanced foreign aerospace systems;
* intelligence collection against military exercises;
* drone incursions into restricted ranges;
* sensor failures or misinterpretation;
* genuine unknowns requiring further analysis.

</div>

Only after rigorous investigation, he maintained, could more unusual hypotheses even be considered responsibly. This reasoning deliberately separated the decision to investigate from any conclusion about extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/the-military-keeps-encountering-ufos-why-doesnt-the-pentagon-care/2018/03/09/242c125c-22ee-11e8-94da-ebf9d112159c_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostThe military keeps encountering UFOs. Why doesn’t the Pentagon care? - The Washington PostMarch 9, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 9, 2018</span></span></span>

That distinction became particularly visible after the 2023 Chinese surveillance balloon incident. Mellon observed that the episode demonstrated how unidentified objects in national airspace could represent legitimate security concerns without involving alien spacecraft. ## Influence on congressional oversight

Mellon's framing also helped redirect attention towards oversight rather than disclosure alone.

Instead of arguing primarily for revealing hidden secrets, he advocated:

* consistent reporting procedures across the armed services;
* all-source intelligence analysis combining multiple sensor systems;
* stronger [congressional oversight]({{ 'congressional-oversight/' | relative_url }}) of defence reporting;
* improved information sharing between military organisations;
* greater transparency where classification was not genuinely required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://christopherkmellon.substack.com/p/setting-the-record-straight" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: christopherkmellon.substack.com">[christopherkmellon.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">christopherkmellon.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Setting the Record Straight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by Christopher MellonOctober 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 25, 2023</span></span></span>

These briefings helped move discussion from media coverage towards formal legislative interest in reporting standards and intelligence processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://christopherkmellon.substack.com/p/setting-the-record-straight" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: christopherkmellon.substack.com">[christopherkmellon.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">christopherkmellon.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Setting the Record Straight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by Christopher MellonOctober 25, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 25, 2023</span></span></span>

## Limits and continuing debate

Mellon's framing has attracted both support and criticism.

Supporters argue that he successfully separated the question of whether unusual aerial incidents deserve investigation from the far more difficult question of whether they involve non-human intelligence. They credit this distinction with helping reduce the stigma surrounding military reporting and encouraging more systematic oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/the-military-keeps-encountering-ufos-why-doesnt-the-pentagon-care/2018/03/09/242c125c-22ee-11e8-94da-ebf9d112159c_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostThe military keeps encountering UFOs. Why doesn’t the Pentagon care? - The Washington PostMarch 9, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 9, 2018</span></span></span>

Critics respond that Mellon's public advocacy sometimes sat alongside individuals making much broader claims about hidden crash-retrieval programmes or extraterrestrial technology, potentially blurring the line between evidence-based security concerns and speculative narratives. Others note that official reviews, including the Pentagon's 2024 historical assessment, concluded that available government investigations had found no verified evidence of extraterrestrial technology while still recognising the need to evaluate unidentified reports carefully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2024/03/08/no-ufo-aliens-pentagon-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostPentagon report finds no evidence of alien visits, hidden spacecraft - The Washington Post...</span></span></span>

The lasting significance of Mellon's contribution therefore lies less in any claim about what UAP ultimately are than in how the subject came to be discussed. By redefining UFO reports as questions of airspace awareness, intelligence collection and civilian oversight, he provided a framework that allowed governments to investigate unexplained incidents without requiring acceptance of extraordinary conclusions.
