## Introduction

The strongest lesson from the US Department of Defense's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office ([AARO]({{ 'aaro/' | relative_url }})) is not simply that many reported unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) turn out to be ordinary objects. It is that looking only at the remaining unexplained reports creates a powerful form of selection bias. When resolved cases quietly disappear from public discussion while unresolved ones dominate headlines, the residual set can appear far more extraordinary than the full body of evidence actually supports.

This matters when celebrity commentators, [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}) or advocacy groups highlight only the most mysterious incidents. AARO's published case resolutions provide an important corrective: they show how additional data, better sensor analysis and contextual investigation frequently transform apparently unusual sightings into balloons, drones, satellites, aircraft, birds or [sensor effects]({{ 'sensor-effects/' | relative_url }}). The unresolved category is therefore not a collection of confirmed anomalies, but a temporary holding category whose composition changes as evidence improves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## What the closed-case data actually contains

AARO's annual reporting distinguishes between cases that remain under investigation and cases that have been formally resolved. The closed cases are notable because they consistently resolve into familiar categories rather than exotic technology.

## Why solved reports receive far less publicity

Selection bias emerges because resolved reports rarely generate the same attention as unresolved ones.

Several forces reinforce this imbalance:

* **Narrative appeal.** A mystery attracts more attention than a balloon identified after technical analysis.
* **Media incentives.** Dramatic uncertainty produces headlines, whereas ordinary explanations are less likely to receive follow-up coverage.
* **Psychological salience.** People remember spectacular unresolved stories more readily than routine investigative outcomes.
* **Advocacy incentives.** Campaigns arguing that governments possess evidence of extraordinary craft naturally emphasise [unresolved cases]({{ 'unresolved-cases/' | relative_url }}) rather than the much larger body of mundane identifications.

This imbalance means many audiences become familiar with famous incidents while remaining unaware that investigators routinely close other reports through conventional analysis.

For celebrity-led UFO projects, the effect can be particularly pronounced. Public presentations often revolve around the small number of unresolved military videos or eyewitness accounts because these are more compelling than discussing dozens of investigations ending with balloons, drones or imaging artefacts. The resulting picture is incomplete even when every individual example is presented accurately.

## How residual cases can be made to look exceptional

A classic statistical mistake is to evaluate only the cases that survive an initial filtering process.

Imagine a reporting system receiving hundreds of observations. As better evidence becomes available, investigators eliminate many through ordinary explanations. The remaining cases naturally become more unusual—not necessarily because they represent exotic phenomena, but because all the straightforward explanations have already been removed.

This produces what statisticians recognise as a selection effect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Large numbers of mixed-quality reports enter the system.
2. Cases with sufficient evidence are identified as ordinary.
3. Only difficult, poorly documented or information-limited reports remain.
4. The remaining subset appears disproportionately mysterious because the ordinary cases have already been filtered out.

</div>

The unresolved pool therefore should not be treated as representative of all reported UAP.

NASA's independent UAP study made a similar methodological point. It concluded that limited, poorly characterised observations prevent reliable scientific conclusions and argued that higher-quality, standardised data collection is the primary route to resolving more reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Closed cases change how famous incidents should be interpreted

The existence of many solved investigations does not automatically explain every famous UAP case. Some reports genuinely remain unresolved because available evidence is incomplete.

However, the closed-case record changes the baseline expectation.

Instead of asking, "Why has this case never been explained?", investigators first ask whether the available information resembles other reports that eventually proved to involve:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* incomplete sensor data;
* unusual viewing geometry;
* ordinary aircraft under uncommon conditions;
* balloons;
* drones;
* satellites; or
* environmental or instrumental effects.

</div>

That approach reflects standard investigative practice. A report should move towards increasingly unusual explanations only after more conventional possibilities have been rigorously excluded using sufficient evidence.

## Why this matters when celebrities frame UFO evidence

Within celebrity-driven UFO discussions, unresolved cases often become symbolic evidence for a much broader conclusion. Yet AARO's closed-case record demonstrates why caution is necessary.

If audiences see only the unresolved fraction, they can mistakenly conclude that unexplained reports dominate government investigations. In reality, official reporting shows the opposite pattern: every formally resolved case in recent annual reporting has been attributed to ordinary causes, while many remaining investigations continue simply because investigators lack enough information to reach a confident conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://newparadigminstitute.org/learn/library/aaro-fy24-consolidated-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newparadigminstitute.org">[newparadigminstitute.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newparadigminstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aaro fy24 consolidated report – New Paradigm Institute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 14, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2024</span></span></span>

This does not prove that every future report will receive a conventional explanation. Nor does it establish that all historical claims are incorrect. What it does show is that the investigative process consistently removes many apparently mysterious sightings through careful analysis, leaving behind a non-random subset that is inherently more difficult to explain.

Understanding that filtering process helps guard against one of the most common reasoning errors in UFO debates: treating the unexplained remainder as though it represents the whole dataset rather than the cases that have simply not yet reached a satisfactory evidential conclusion.
