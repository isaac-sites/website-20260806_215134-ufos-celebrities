## Introduction

The publication of three U.S. Navy infrared videos in December 2017 marked a turning point in the modern UFO debate because the footage did not reach the public in isolation. Instead, Tom DeLonge's newly formed [To The Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) of Arts & Science (TTSA) coordinated the release alongside named military witnesses, former senior defence and intelligence officials, and reporting by *The New York Times*. This combination transformed material that might otherwise have remained a niche internet story into a mainstream news event. The campaign did not prove that the recorded objects were extraterrestrial, nor did it resolve what the objects were. Its significance lay in changing how journalists, policymakers and much of the public approached the subject: from fringe speculation towards questions of military aviation safety, intelligence oversight and unexplained aerial encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. ## Packaging footage, witnesses and official biographies

The three videos—later widely known as **FLIR1**, **Gimbal** and **GoFast**—showed infrared targeting-camera recordings made during separate U.S. Navy encounters in 2004 and 2015. By themselves, the clips were brief, grainy and open to multiple interpretations. Their impact came from how they were presented.

TTSA assembled several elements that reinforced one another:

* **Military footage** originating from genuine Navy aircraft.
* **Named pilots**, including Commander David Fravor, who publicly described one of the encounters.
* **Former Pentagon and intelligence officials**, notably Christopher Mellon and Luis Elizondo, whose government backgrounds signalled that the story deserved attention beyond UFO enthusiast circles.
* **Major newspaper reporting**, particularly *The New York Times* investigation published on 16 December 2017, which linked the videos to the previously little-known Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. This packaging strategy mattered because mainstream editors could treat the story as an investigation into defence policy rather than a claim about alien spacecraft. The credibility of identifiable witnesses and former officials reduced the reputational risk of covering a topic that had long been associated with sensationalism.

Christopher Mellon played a particularly important role by helping bring military material into the public discussion while arguing that unidentified aerial incidents deserved official attention regardless of their ultimate explanation. That framing aligned with conventional national-security reporting rather than paranormal journalism.

## Why military imagery carried unusual media weight

The 2017 campaign succeeded partly because military sensor recordings are viewed differently from ordinary civilian UFO photographs.

Several characteristics made the [Navy videos]({{ 'navy-videos/' | relative_url }}) especially newsworthy:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* They originated from aircraft equipped with sophisticated targeting systems rather than consumer cameras.
* The recordings were linked to identifiable military training events.
* Pilot reactions were preserved in cockpit audio, allowing audiences to hear trained aviators expressing uncertainty in real time.
* The footage could be discussed independently of extraordinary claims about its origin.

</div>

For journalists, these characteristics shifted the central question from "Did someone claim to see a UFO?" to "Why were experienced military personnel unable to identify what they recorded?" That distinction broadened the audience beyond long-time UFO followers.

Equally important was the broader political context. The disclosure coincided with reporting that the Pentagon had funded AATIP, demonstrating that the U.S. government itself had considered unidentified aerial incidents worthy of investigation. Whether or not AATIP's conclusions were controversial, its existence made the underlying story easier for mainstream outlets to justify covering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. ## What the videos did—and did not—demonstrate

One reason the 2017 coverage generated sustained debate is that the videos supported some claims while leaving others unresolved.

They did demonstrate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* That genuine U.S. Navy aircraft recorded unusual objects or phenomena.
* That military personnel regarded the encounters as sufficiently noteworthy to preserve the footage.
* That senior former officials argued the incidents deserved further investigation.

</div>

They did **not** demonstrate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* That the objects were extraterrestrial.
* That they represented technology beyond all known human capability.
* That every reported observation matched the behaviour suggested by popular retellings.
* That the short infrared clips alone provided enough information to determine speed, distance or propulsion with certainty.

</div>

These distinctions are important because much later public discussion blurred the difference between authenticated military footage and explanations of what the footage showed. The Department of Defense's later official release of the three videos in 2020 confirmed that they were authentic Navy recordings, but it explicitly stated only that the observed aerial phenomena remained "unidentified". It did not endorse any specific explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Release...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

## From niche disclosure to mainstream news

Before December 2017, military UFO cases circulated largely within specialist books, [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}) and online communities. After the coordinated release, major newspapers, television networks and political reporters treated the subject as a legitimate national-security issue.

The shift resulted less from the visual quality of the footage than from the surrounding institutional context. TTSA's strategy effectively combined:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* recognisable public figures such as Tom DeLonge;
* former officials with defence credentials;
* identifiable military witnesses;
* documentary evidence in the form of Navy sensor recordings; and
* simultaneous reporting by respected national media.

</div>

Each element compensated for the limitations of the others. Grainy videos gained credibility through named witnesses, while eyewitness testimony gained visibility through widely circulated recordings and investigative journalism.

## The lasting significance of the 2017 release

The lasting importance of the three Navy videos lies less in what they proved than in how they reshaped public conversation.
