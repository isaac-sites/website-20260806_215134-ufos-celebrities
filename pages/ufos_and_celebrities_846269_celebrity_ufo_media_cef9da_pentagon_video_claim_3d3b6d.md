## Introduction

The Pentagon's release and authentication of three well-known US [Navy videos]({{ 'navy-videos/' | relative_url }}) marked an important moment in the public discussion of unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP), formerly called UFOs. However, one of the most common misunderstandings is that the US Department of Defense confirmed the videos showed extraterrestrial spacecraft. It did not. The official confirmation established that the footage was genuine military video recorded by Navy personnel and that the objects in the recordings remained *unidentified*. It did not determine what the objects were or conclude that they were alien technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

This distinction became especially important as celebrities, [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}) and major news outlets amplified the story. Official authentication gave the videos institutional credibility, but it did not resolve the underlying question of the objects' identity. Understanding that difference helps explain how an official government action could increase public interest without providing evidence for extraterrestrial explanations.

## What the Defense Department officially confirmed

In April 2020, the US Department of Defense formally released three unclassified Navy videos commonly known as **FLIR1**, **Gimbal** and **GoFast**. Two had already appeared in reporting by *The New York Times* in 2017, while another had circulated through [To The Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}), the organisation co-founded by musician Tom DeLonge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

The Pentagon's statement was carefully worded. It confirmed that:

* the videos were authentic US Navy recordings;
* they could be released without exposing sensitive military capabilities;
* the footage had previously circulated unofficially; and
* the aerial phenomena shown remained "unidentified".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

Notably absent from the statement was any claim about extraterrestrial origin. The Department of Defense did not describe the objects as alien craft, advanced foreign technology or even unexplained physics. It simply acknowledged that investigators had not identified the objects from the available evidence.

The release therefore authenticated the **source of the footage**, not the **nature of the objects** within it.

## Why "unidentified" does not mean "extraterrestrial"

The word *unidentified* has a precise investigative meaning. It indicates that available evidence is insufficient to determine what an object was with confidence. It does not imply that every conventional explanation has been ruled out.

Military investigators face many reasons why an observation may remain unresolved:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* incomplete sensor recordings;
* limited viewing angles;
* uncertain distance or speed;
* classified contextual information that cannot be released publicly;
* absence of corroborating radar or visual data.

</div>

An unresolved case therefore reflects incomplete evidence rather than proof of an extraordinary explanation.

This distinction is common in aviation and intelligence work. Investigators routinely separate three different questions:

QuestionPossible answerWas the recording genuine?YesWas something observed?YesWhat was it?Unknown

Only the first two questions were answered by the Pentagon's authentication.

Subsequent government investigations have continued to emphasise this distinction. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office ([AARO]({{ 'aaro/' | relative_url }})), established to investigate UAP reports, has repeatedly stated that it follows the evidence wherever it leads but has found no verified evidence that any investigated UAP represented extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3700894/statement-by-pentagon-press-secretary-maj-gen-pat-ryder-on-the-historical-recor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by Pentagon Press Secretary Maj. ## Why authentic military footage still leaves uncertainty

The Navy videos became influential because they combined several features that audiences often associate with credibility:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* trained military pilots;
* advanced infrared targeting systems;
* official military documentation;
* eventual Pentagon confirmation.

</div>

Those characteristics make the footage more significant than anonymous internet videos. They do **not**, however, eliminate the technical difficulties involved in interpreting infrared imagery.

Infrared targeting cameras compress three-dimensional events into two-dimensional recordings. Factors including viewing geometry, sensor settings, aircraft movement and atmospheric conditions can affect how motion appears on screen. Analysts continue to debate aspects of the videos, particularly the apparent movement and rotation seen in the *Gimbal* footage, while acknowledging that the available public data are incomplete. The videos alone do not provide enough information to calculate an object's exact speed, distance or propulsion system with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reconstruction of Potential Flight Paths for the January 2015 Gimbal UAPJune 15, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 15, 2023</span></span></span>

That is why [authentic footage]({{ 'authenticity/' | relative_url }}) can still produce multiple competing interpretations without any single explanation being definitively proven.

## How celebrity coverage widened the official claim

Within the broader story of celebrities and UFOs, the Pentagon's authentication became a powerful media amplifier because it supplied an official anchor for an already high-profile narrative.

Tom DeLonge and To The Stars Academy had helped bring the videos into public discussion before the Pentagon's formal release. Once the Department of Defense confirmed the recordings were genuine Navy videos, many headlines understandably focused on the unprecedented official acknowledgement. Yet some public discussion blurred an important distinction:

* **accurate claim:** the Pentagon confirmed the videos were authentic military recordings;
* **incorrect inference:** the Pentagon confirmed aliens.

As celebrities, television documentaries, podcasts and social media personalities repeated the story, the institutional authority of the Pentagon often became shorthand for a much stronger conclusion than officials had actually reached. The official statement was comparatively narrow, but repeated retelling sometimes transformed "officially released" into "officially confirmed UFOs" and then, in some discussions, into "officially confirmed alien spacecraft."

This illustrates how celebrity-driven media ecosystems can expand the perceived significance of an official action without changing what the evidence itself demonstrates.

## Later investigations reinforced the same distinction

Since the videos were officially released, the US government has expanded rather than abandoned its investigation of unidentified aerial phenomena. Congressional interest led to new reporting mechanisms and the creation of AARO to examine military reports using scientific and intelligence methods.

Despite taking the subject seriously, the Department of Defense has consistently maintained that official investigation should not be confused with confirmation of extraterrestrial technology. In its 2024 historical review, AARO reported that it found no verifiable evidence that any US government investigation had confirmed a UAP sighting as extraterrestrial, nor evidence that the government possessed recovered alien technology. Most historical reports were assessed as ordinary objects, [sensor effects]({{ 'sensor-effects/' | relative_url }}) or misidentifications, while some cases remained unresolved because of insufficient data rather than evidence of alien origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/3701297/dod-report-discounts-sightings-of-extraterrestrial-technology/)

The Pentagon's position has therefore remained remarkably consistent from the original video release onward: authentic military recordings deserve careful investigation, but authenticity of the footage should not be mistaken for proof of what the recorded objects actually were.
