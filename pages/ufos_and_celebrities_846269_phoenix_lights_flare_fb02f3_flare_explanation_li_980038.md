## Introduction

The military confirmation that illumination flares were dropped during the [Phoenix Lights]({{ 'phoenix-lights/' | relative_url }}) incident is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the wider debate, but it is often misunderstood. The confirmation established a plausible and well-supported explanation for the later display of bright lights filmed south-west of Phoenix. It did **not** establish that every sighting reported under the label "Phoenix Lights" had the same origin. This distinction matters because the term *Phoenix Lights* has come to describe multiple reports from different times, locations and viewing directions on the evening of 13 March 1997. Understanding exactly what the military confirmed—and what remained outside that confirmation—is essential for interpreting the evidence fairly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2016/11/the-phoenix-lights-become-an-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

## Where the Flare Explanation Reaches Its Limit

The official military account concerned a specific training activity over the Barry M. Goldwater Range. Visiting Maryland Air National Guard A-10 aircraft released high-intensity illumination flares during exercises, producing a line of bright lights visible from the Phoenix metropolitan area. This explanation closely matches the widely broadcast footage showing lights apparently hovering before disappearing one by one behind the Sierra Estrella mountains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2016/11/the-phoenix-lights-become-an-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

However, the official confirmation was never presented as a universal explanation for every report from that evening. Contemporary reporting noted that military spokespeople linked the flare release to the later lights, while also recognising that earlier reports from different parts of Arizona described something different in both timing and appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/news/the-hack-and-the-quack-6445593/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenixnewtimes.com">[Phoenix New Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenixnewtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix New Times The Hack and the Quack &#124; Phoenix New Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix New TimesThe Hack and the Quack &#124; Phoenix New TimesMarch 5, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 5, 1998</span></span></span>

The distinction is important because evidence is only as strong as the claim it supports. A confirmed explanation for one event does not automatically become evidence that every superficially similar report shares the same cause.

## The Exact Claims Confirmed by Military Spokespeople

The military confirmation established several specific points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A-10 aircraft from the Maryland Air National Guard were operating in Arizona during the exercise.
* Illumination flares were released over the Barry M. Goldwater Range.
* The flare release occurred late in the evening, matching the timing of the stationary lights recorded on numerous camcorders.
* The behaviour of the lights—remaining nearly stationary before disappearing sequentially—was consistent with descending parachute flares becoming hidden behind distant terrain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2016/11/the-phoenix-lights-become-an-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[skepticalinquirer.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

</div>

These are substantial findings because they identify a specific aircraft type, operating unit, training area and military activity rather than offering a vague suggestion that "it might have been military exercises."

What they do **not** establish is that every witness elsewhere in Arizona observed those same flares.

## Reports Outside the Relevant Time and Viewing Direction

Many discussions blur together reports that occurred at different times.

Numerous witnesses described a moving V-shaped or chevron formation earlier in the evening, before the later stationary display became widely filmed. Reports of this earlier event came from locations extending well beyond metropolitan Phoenix, with observations occurring along a route across Arizona rather than from a single viewing area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/arts-culture/phoenix-lights-ufo-mystery-explanations-19105870/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenixnewtimes.com">[Phoenix New Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenixnewtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix New Times Phoenix Lights UFO mystery explanations &#124; Phoenix New Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix New Times Phoenix Lights UFO mystery explanations &#124; Phoenix New Times</span></span></span>

Whether those earlier reports represented one phenomenon, multiple aircraft, observational error or something else is a separate question. The military flare confirmation neither proves nor disproves any of those possibilities because it addresses a different event.

Likewise, witnesses viewing different parts of the sky cannot automatically be assumed to have observed the same object. A confirmed flare release over the south-western horizon does not explain reports describing overhead movement in other directions unless additional evidence links them together.

## Why a Strong Partial Explanation Is Not a Total Solution

A common reasoning error in discussions of the Phoenix Lights is treating partial explanations as complete ones.

The flare evidence is persuasive because multiple independent lines of evidence converge:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* official confirmation of the exercise;
* the correct location over a known military range;
* timing consistent with the later recordings;
* behaviour matching descending illumination flares; and
* terrain analysis showing the lights disappearing behind mountain ridges rather than extinguishing simultaneously in open sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/arts-culture/the-phoenix-lights-are-no-mystery-6661825/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenixnewtimes.com">[Phoenix New Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenixnewtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the phoenix lights are no mystery 6661825</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix New TimesThe 1997 &quot;Phoenix Lights&quot; are no mystery &#124; Phoenix New Times...</span></span></span>

</div>

Yet none of those observations demonstrate that every witness account from earlier in the evening referred to the same display.

Conversely, recognising that the flare explanation does not cover every report does not mean the remaining reports represent extraordinary phenomena. It simply means that each group of observations requires its own evidence and should be evaluated independently.

## Why Separating the Events Improves the Debate

Treating the Phoenix Lights as a single, indivisible incident encourages false choices: either the military explanation explains everything, or it explains nothing.

A more evidence-based approach separates the reports into distinct observational episodes and asks what evidence exists for each one individually. This allows strong conclusions where the evidence is strong—such as the later flare display—while avoiding claims that exceed the available evidence for earlier reports.

This distinction has become widely recognised in both sceptical analyses and more sympathetic discussions of the case.
