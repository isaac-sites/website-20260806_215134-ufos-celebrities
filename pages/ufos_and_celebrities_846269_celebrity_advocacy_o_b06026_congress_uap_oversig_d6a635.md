## Introduction

From 2020 onward, Congress transformed unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) from an intermittent media topic into a continuing matter of government oversight. Rather than asking whether extraordinary claims about UFOs were true, lawmakers increasingly focused on a narrower question: was the United States government collecting, analysing and reporting unusual military encounters in a consistent and accountable way? That shift reframed the issue around aviation safety, intelligence collection, national security and public transparency. It also created a regular cycle of intelligence reports, public hearings and statutory reporting requirements that outlasted the initial burst of media attention surrounding military videos and [celebrity advocacy]({{ 'official-actions/' | relative_url }}).

This institutional response did not validate claims of extraterrestrial technology. Instead, Congress used its constitutional oversight powers to require information from defence and intelligence agencies, examine reporting failures and press officials on how unexplained incidents were investigated. In doing so, UAPs became part of routine congressional scrutiny rather than an occasional cultural controversy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf?stream=science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prelimary Assessment UAP 20210625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ODNIUNCLASSIFIEDJune 25, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2021</span></span></span>

## The intelligence assessment ordered by Congress

The decisive turning point came when the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence directed the Director of National Intelligence (DNI), in consultation with the Secretary of Defense, to produce a public assessment of what the government knew about UAPs. This instruction accompanied the Intelligence Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2021 and reflected growing concern that military personnel were reporting encounters without a standard process for collecting or analysing information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf?stream=science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prelimary Assessment UAP 20210625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ODNIUNCLASSIFIEDJune 25, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2021</span></span></span>

The resulting **Preliminary Assessment: Unidentified Aerial Phenomena**, released in June 2021, represented an important institutional milestone. It did not argue for any extraordinary explanation. Instead, it highlighted practical weaknesses:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* reporting across government was fragmented and inconsistent;
* most cases lacked sufficient data for confident identification;
* several incidents involved multiple sensors, suggesting they warranted further analysis rather than immediate dismissal;
* unidentified objects could potentially represent aviation hazards or foreign intelligence activities.

</div>

The report examined 144 military reports collected between 2004 and March 2021. Only one case was confidently identified, while the remainder remained unresolved primarily because of limited or inconsistent information rather than because investigators concluded they represented advanced or non-human technology. The report repeatedly stressed the need for better collection methods, standardised reporting and improved analytical processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf?stream=science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prelimary Assessment UAP 20210625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ODNIUNCLASSIFIEDJune 25, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2021</span></span></span>

This distinction mattered politically. Congress was not asking agencies to prove extraordinary claims. Instead, legislators argued that incomplete reporting itself represented an accountability problem requiring oversight.

## Why public hearings changed the subject's legitimacy

Congressional hearings altered the public conversation by moving discussion of UAPs from [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}), podcasts and television interviews into formal oversight proceedings conducted under oath.

The first major open House hearing in May 2022 featured senior Pentagon intelligence officials explaining how the Department of Defense investigated military encounters. Members of Congress questioned officials about reporting procedures, sensor analysis, intelligence sharing and whether pilots still feared professional stigma when reporting unusual incidents. The hearing focused less on speculation about extraterrestrial life than on whether government institutions had adequate systems for recognising potential security threats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Congressional-Press-Products/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Congressional/Press Products</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO Congressional/Press Products...</span></span></span>

A second highly publicised House hearing in July 2023 expanded the debate. Witnesses included former intelligence officer David Grusch alongside former Navy aviators Ryan Graves and David Fravor. Grusch alleged that the government had concealed programmes involving recovered UAP materials, while the pilots described repeated operational encounters with unidentified objects. These allegations attracted worldwide attention, but Congress treated them primarily as oversight questions requiring investigation rather than accepted factual findings. Pentagon officials subsequently stated that they had found no verifiable evidence supporting claims of secret programmes recovering extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/6298287/congress-ufo-hearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">intelligence official, testified before Congress, alleging that the U.S. government has been concealing a longstanding program focused on...</span></span></span>

The hearings therefore served two purposes simultaneously:

* they legitimised discussion of military reporting failures;
* they subjected extraordinary claims to formal questioning instead of leaving them within popular media.

That procedural shift significantly changed public perception. Congressional committees were investigating whether government processes were adequate, not endorsing any single explanation for the reported phenomena.

## Oversight became a continuing responsibility

Congress reinforced oversight by embedding UAP requirements into defence legislation rather than treating them as one-off investigations.

Annual reporting requirements encouraged agencies to provide regular updates rather than isolated assessments. Lawmakers also supported the establishment of the **All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office ([AARO]({{ 'aaro/' | relative_url }}))** in 2022, replacing earlier investigative structures with a permanent office responsible for examining anomalous objects observed across air, sea, space and other operational domains. Congress required classified briefings, unclassified reports and continuing engagement between AARO and oversight committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Next-AARO-Home-redesign/Next-Parent/AARO-Congressional-Tab-DT/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Congressional/Press Products</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO Congressional/Press Products...</span></span></span>

This legislative framework reflected several recurring concerns:

* ensuring military personnel could report unusual encounters without stigma;
* improving data collection across military services;
* distinguishing genuine anomalies from drones, [sensor artefacts]({{ 'sensor-artefacts/' | relative_url }}) or foreign surveillance systems;
* providing Congress with regular information sufficient to exercise budgetary and policy oversight.

By making reporting a statutory requirement, Congress ensured that attention to UAPs would not depend entirely on media interest or changes in executive priorities.

## Transparency demands and the limits of public evidence

Congressional oversight consistently balanced two competing objectives: increasing transparency while recognising the limits imposed by classified information and national security.

Many legislators argued that excessive secrecy undermined public confidence, particularly if agencies appeared reluctant to explain unusual military encounters. ## How this changed the role of celebrity advocacy

Within the broader story of [UFOs and celebrities]({{ 'ufos-and-celebrities/' | relative_url }}), congressional oversight illustrates where public advocacy ended and institutional accountability began.

High-profile figures helped attract public attention to military encounters and allegations of government secrecy, creating political momentum for further questions. However, Congress generally avoided making policy around celebrity claims themselves. Instead, lawmakers concentrated on whether defence and intelligence agencies had reliable reporting systems, appropriate investigative structures and sufficient accountability to elected representatives.

This distinction remains central to understanding official UAP policy. Celebrity advocacy contributed to visibility, but Congress converted that visibility into a governance issue by demanding intelligence assessments, creating recurring reporting obligations and holding public hearings focused on national security, transparency and government performance. The result was not official endorsement of extraordinary explanations, but the normalisation of UAPs as a legitimate subject of congressional oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf?stream=science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[dni.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Prelimary Assessment UAP 20210625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ODNIUNCLASSIFIEDJune 25, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2021</span></span></span>
