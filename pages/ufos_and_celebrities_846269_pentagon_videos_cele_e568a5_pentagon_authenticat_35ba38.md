## Introduction

When the Pentagon formally released three US [Navy videos]({{ 'navy-videos/' | relative_url }}) in April 2020, it confirmed something important but much narrower than many headlines implied. The Department of Defense verified that the footage was genuine military material recorded by Navy personnel, authorised its official public release, and stated that the objects seen in the videos remained *unidentified*. It did **not** conclude that the objects were extraterrestrial, that they displayed impossible technology, or that the videos proved extraordinary flight performance. The distinction matters because much of the later public discussion—especially in popular culture and celebrity-driven UFO promotion—treated official authentication as if it were official endorsement of the most dramatic interpretations. The Pentagon's statement did not make that leap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

## The April 2020 release and its exact wording

On 27 April 2020, the Department of Defense issued a short press statement announcing the authorised release of three unclassified Navy videos: one recorded in November 2004 and two recorded in January 2015. The videos had already circulated publicly following unauthorised disclosures in 2007 and 2017, and the Navy had previously acknowledged that they were genuine Navy recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

That final sentence is often quoted incompletely. It confirmed the status of the observations within the investigation—not the nature or origin of the objects themselves.

## What authenticity actually established about the recordings

The Pentagon's authentication settled several factual questions that had circulated for years.

It established that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the three videos were genuine recordings made by US Navy aircraft sensors;
* the released files were authentic government material rather than fabricated or digitally manipulated clips;
* the Department of Defense had authorised their public release after review;
* military personnel had regarded the observed objects as unidentified at the time represented by those recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[defense.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>

</div>

These are meaningful confirmations. Before the official release, debate often centred on whether the videos themselves were genuine. The Pentagon removed that uncertainty.

However, [authenticity]({{ 'authenticity/' | relative_url }}) of a recording is different from authenticity of an interpretation. A genuine infrared video can faithfully record an event while still leaving open what produced the image or how particular visual effects should be interpreted.

## What the official statement did not support

The Department of Defense deliberately avoided making claims that later became common in public discussion.

It did **not** state that:

* the objects were alien spacecraft;
* they represented technology beyond known human capabilities;
* they demonstrated impossible acceleration or instantaneous manoeuvres;
* they violated known laws of physics;
* every apparent movement visible in the videos reflected the object's true motion rather than [sensor effects]({{ 'sensor-effects/' | relative_url }}), viewing geometry or incomplete information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[defense.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>

The word *unidentified* simply means investigators had not reached a confirmed identification using the available information. In aviation and defence contexts, an unidentified object is not automatically mysterious in origin; it is an object whose identity has not yet been established with sufficient confidence.

This distinction is reflected in later government reporting, which consistently treated "unidentified" as an investigative category rather than evidence of extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rele...</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3700894/statement-by-pentagon-press-secretary-maj-gen-pat-ryder-on-the-historical-recor/)

## Why official authentication was widely misunderstood

Part of the confusion arose because several different questions became merged into one.

One question asked whether the videos themselves were genuine. The Pentagon answered yes.

Another asked whether the objects remained unidentified in official records. The Pentagon again answered yes.

A third question asked what the objects actually were. The Pentagon gave no answer.

In media coverage and online discussion, these separate issues were often compressed into a simpler narrative: "The Pentagon confirmed UFOs." That shorthand blurred the distinction between confirming the authenticity of evidence and confirming the extraordinary interpretation of that evidence. Reports explaining the release generally noted that the Department of Defense had authenticated the footage while stopping short of identifying the phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcnews.com/Politics/pentagon-declassifies-navy-videos-purportedly-show-ufos/story?id=70364183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcnews.com">[abcnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News Pentagon declassifies Navy videos that purportedly show UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsPentagon declassifies Navy videos that purportedly show UFOs - ABC News...</span></span></span>

## Why the distinction matters when evaluating the Navy videos

The three videos remain significant because they document real military encounters that officials considered worthy of investigation. Authentic military recordings deserve careful analysis.

At the same time, authentication does not resolve longstanding debates over what the clips show. Researchers, aviation specialists and independent analysts have continued to examine issues such as infrared imaging, targeting-pod behaviour, range uncertainty, viewing angles and possible optical effects. Some argue that aspects of the videos can be explained through ordinary aircraft, distant objects or sensor geometry, while others believe important questions remain unresolved because the publicly released clips lack accompanying radar data, telemetry and classified contextual information. The Pentagon's 2020 statement did not attempt to settle those technical disputes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/does-it-matter-that-the-dod-released-those-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These videos, previously surfaced through The New York Times in 2017, were not initially authorized by the Pentagon, though they were ack...</span></span></span>
