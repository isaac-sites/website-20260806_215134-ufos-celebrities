## Introduction

[To the Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) of Arts & Science (TTSA) attracted unusual attention because it paired a globally recognised celebrity, Tom DeLonge, with former senior figures from the US defence, intelligence and aerospace communities. That combination gave the organisation a level of public credibility rarely seen in modern UFO advocacy. Instead of asking audiences to trust anonymous insiders or speculative researchers, TTSA presented individuals whose careers had included work at the CIA, the Pentagon and Lockheed Martin's Skunk Works. For many journalists and members of the public, those professional backgrounds made the organisation appear unusually authoritative, even though former government service did not, by itself, verify TTSA's claims about unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) or advanced technology. The distinction between personal expertise and evidential proof became one of the defining tensions in TTSA's public impact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_The_Stars_Inc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">To The Stars Inc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">To The Stars Inc</span></span></span>

Among its best-known figures were:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Tom DeLonge**, musician and long-time public advocate for UFO disclosure, who supplied a large existing audience and media profile.
* **Jim Semivan**, a retired senior CIA operations officer whose intelligence career gave the organisation immediate institutional credibility.
* **Harold E. Puthoff**, a physicist with decades of work in government-funded research programmes, including classified contracts, although he was also known for controversial investigations into paranormal subjects.
* **Luis Elizondo**, a former Department of Defense counter-intelligence official who stated that he had led the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), making him the public face of claims that the US government had taken UAP seriously.
* **Christopher Mellon**, former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and a member of the Mellon family, who had extensive experience in defence oversight and congressional affairs.
* **Steve Justice**, formerly Director of Advanced Systems at Lockheed Martin's Skunk Works, bringing recognised aerospace engineering credentials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_The_Stars_Inc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">To The Stars Inc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">To The Stars Inc</span></span></span>

</div>

Each individual represented a different source of institutional legitimacy. Intelligence experience suggested privileged access to classified processes, aerospace engineering implied technical expertise, and senior defence positions conveyed familiarity with national security decision-making. Collectively, the team projected an image that differed sharply from earlier UFO organisations built primarily around civilian investigators or enthusiasts.

## Why professional backgrounds impressed journalists

TTSA's personnel mattered because news organisations routinely evaluate sources partly through demonstrated expertise and institutional experience. Former officials cannot reveal classified information at will, but recognised careers can indicate familiarity with government procedures, military reporting systems and intelligence analysis.

For journalists covering an unfamiliar subject, TTSA offered identifiable, on-the-record sources with verifiable employment histories. This reduced one of the traditional barriers to reporting on UFO claims. Instead of relying on anonymous witnesses or fringe researchers, reporters could interview former officials who had held security clearances and senior responsibilities. That helped mainstream outlets frame UAP as a legitimate national security issue rather than solely a question of extraterrestrial speculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">what is up with those pentagon ufo videos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. The organisation also benefited from what communication scholars often describe as **borrowed credibility**: audiences transferred trust from respected institutions to individuals formerly associated with them. A former CIA officer or Pentagon official speaking about unexplained aerial incidents naturally carried greater perceived authority than an ordinary civilian, even when speaking in a private capacity.

This mechanism did not require audiences to believe every claim. Rather, it encouraged many observers to conclude that the subject itself deserved more serious attention because people with substantial government careers considered it worthy of investigation.

## How credibility amplified TTSA's message

The backgrounds of TTSA's former officials strengthened several aspects of its public campaign.

First, they made the organisation appear connected to genuine government discussions about UAP. Even without revealing classified information, former officials could explain how military reporting worked, describe bureaucratic obstacles and argue that pilots' observations should be examined rather than dismissed.

Second, their participation reinforced the significance of the [Navy videos]({{ 'navy-videos/' | relative_url }}) released during TTSA's early years. Viewers were not simply watching infrared footage; they were hearing interpretations from people presented as having direct experience with defence intelligence and military systems. That combination increased media interest and encouraged policymakers to treat the issue as potentially relevant to aviation safety and national security rather than entertainment alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">what is up with those pentagon ufo videos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. Third, the team's diversity mattered. Christopher Mellon's policy experience, Steve Justice's aerospace background and Jim Semivan's intelligence career suggested that concern about UAP crossed professional boundaries instead of originating from a single niche community.

## Where credentials stopped short of verifying claims

Professional reputations increased TTSA's visibility, but they did not establish the truth of its more ambitious claims.

A former intelligence officer does not automatically possess comprehensive knowledge of every classified programme, nor does previous employment provide independent evidence for extraordinary assertions. Scientific and historical claims still require documentation, reproducible evidence and independent scrutiny.

This distinction became especially important because TTSA's public messaging sometimes moved beyond documented military encounters into broader suggestions about exotic technologies, recovered materials and revolutionary aerospace concepts. Those claims generally lacked publicly available evidence comparable to the strength of the founders' professional résumés.

Journalists and researchers increasingly separated two different questions:

* whether former officials genuinely held the positions described; and
* whether their conclusions about UAP were sufficiently supported by publicly available evidence.

The first question was often easier to answer than the second.

## The debate over Luis Elizondo's official role

The clearest example of the limits of borrowed credibility concerned Luis Elizondo.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Elizondo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Luis Elizondo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Luis Elizondo</span></span></span>

Elizondo consistently maintained that he had led AATIP before resigning from the Department of Defense in 2017. His account was supported by several former officials and by figures such as former Senator Harry Reid. However, Pentagon statements over several years have varied, at times confirming aspects of his involvement while at other times stating that he had no assigned responsibilities for AATIP. These contradictory official accounts have fuelled an extended public dispute rather than producing a definitive resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Elizondo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Luis Elizondo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Luis Elizondo</span></span></span>

The controversy illustrates an important point about TTSA's credibility. Even when a spokesperson has genuine government experience, disputes over the precise scope of their former responsibilities can affect how audiences interpret their later claims. The debate has centred less on whether Elizondo worked in defence intelligence—which is well established—and more on exactly what authority he exercised within the Pentagon's UAP activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Elizondo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Luis Elizondo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Luis Elizondo</span></span></span>

## A lasting change in how UFO advocates were perceived

TTSA demonstrated that [celebrity advocacy]({{ 'official-actions/' | relative_url }}) could become significantly more influential when combined with recognised institutional experience. Tom DeLonge's public profile attracted attention, but the participation of former defence, intelligence and aerospace officials persuaded many journalists, policymakers and members of the public that unidentified aerial phenomena deserved more serious consideration than they had previously received.

At the same time, TTSA exposed the limits of institutional authority. Former officials could open doors to mainstream discussion and encourage closer examination of military reports, but their backgrounds could not substitute for independently verifiable evidence. The organisation's greatest contribution to the UFO debate therefore lay less in proving extraordinary claims than in changing who was considered credible enough to raise the questions.

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
