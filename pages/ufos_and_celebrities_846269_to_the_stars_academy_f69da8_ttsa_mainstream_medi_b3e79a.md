## Introduction

[To the Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) (TTSA) did not make unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) a mainstream topic by producing decisive proof of extraordinary claims. Its breakthrough was communicative rather than scientific. By combining a celebrity founder, former defence and intelligence officials, authenticated military imagery, pilot testimony and carefully timed engagement with major news organisations, TTSA helped transform UAP reporting from a niche subject associated with paranormal culture into a story discussed in terms of aviation safety, national security and government transparency. That shift altered both the language and the audience of the debate, encouraging mainstream journalists, policymakers and the public to treat at least some UAP reports as legitimate subjects for investigation rather than automatic dismissal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/navy-confirms-ufo-videos-real" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">navy confirms ufo videos real</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy Confirms UFO Videos Are Real and Show Unidentified Aerial Phenomena &#124; HISTORYSeptember 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2019</span></span></span>

## Building a media-ready UAP narrative

TTSA's central innovation was not discovering new incidents but presenting existing material in a form that mainstream journalism could readily report.

Before 2017, military UFO reports, leaked videos and [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts/' | relative_url }}) already existed, but they were scattered across internet forums, declassified document collections and specialist UFO publications. Most lacked institutional context or recognised spokespeople, making them difficult for large news organisations to verify or present to broad audiences.

TTSA assembled several elements that were rarely combined in one public campaign:

Equally important, TTSA framed unidentified objects as "unidentified" rather than as confirmed extraterrestrial craft. Although many supporters speculated about non-human origins, the organisation's most successful public messaging emphasised that unexplained military encounters deserved investigation regardless of their ultimate explanation. That framing proved far more compatible with conventional journalism.

## The role of pilots, former officials and major reporting

The decisive moment came in late 2017, when reporting on the Pentagon's Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) appeared alongside discussion of Navy infrared videos that TTSA promoted through its platform and public campaign. The combination of official documents, named officials and military footage gave editors multiple independently newsworthy elements rather than relying on extraordinary claims alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. Several groups reinforced one another.

### Former officials provided institutional credibility

Luis Elizondo, Christopher Mellon, Jim Semivan and other former national security figures became important because journalists could identify their previous government roles. Their backgrounds did not prove their interpretations of UAP, but they changed the threshold for newsroom interest.

Instead of interviewing only civilian UFO researchers, reporters could question people who had worked inside the defence and intelligence community. That shifted coverage from entertainment pages towards defence, politics and investigative reporting.

### Military pilots supplied first-hand testimony

Accounts from aviators such as Commander David Fravor and later Ryan Graves gave audiences witnesses whose professional training depended on accurate observation.

The reporting increasingly centred on questions such as:

* What did trained pilots observe?
* Why were these encounters occurring in military training areas?
* Were reporting systems adequate?
* Could unidentified objects represent foreign technology or aviation hazards?

</div>

Those questions appealed to mainstream readers without requiring acceptance of extraordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/navy-confirms-ufo-videos-real" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">navy confirms ufo videos real</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy Confirms UFO Videos Are Real and Show Unidentified Aerial Phenomena &#124; HISTORYSeptember 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2019</span></span></span>

### Major newspapers changed the audience

National newspapers and broadcast organisations translated specialised UAP material into conventional investigative journalism.

Once respected outlets treated the subject as a defence or oversight story, other media organisations could follow without appearing to endorse fringe beliefs. Coverage increasingly focused on government processes, military reporting and intelligence questions rather than alien visitation.

This created a feedback loop: mainstream reporting generated wider public interest, which encouraged further official responses and additional reporting.

## How authenticated Navy videos changed the conversation

The infrared videos commonly known as *FLIR1*, *Gimbal* and *GoFast* became the campaign's strongest visual assets.

Their influence came less from what they conclusively proved than from what they undeniably were: genuine military recordings of encounters that had not received immediate public explanations.

Years later, the Department of Defense formally authorised release of the three videos after confirming they were authentic Navy recordings. The department emphasised that the videos showed unidentified aerial phenomena but did not establish what the objects were or imply extraterrestrial origins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Statement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Rel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarStatement by the Department of Defense on the Release of Historical Navy Videos &gt; U.S. Department of Defense &gt; Release</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/2165713/statement-by-the-department-of-defens/statement-by-the-department-of-defense-on-the-release-of-historical-navy-videos/)

That distinction mattered.

Instead of debating whether the footage itself was fabricated, journalists could focus on questions including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* why military personnel considered the incidents noteworthy;
* how unusual encounters were reported internally;
* whether additional sensor data existed;
* what government agencies were doing to investigate them.

</div>

The discussion therefore shifted from proving that videos were "real" to understanding what the recordings actually represented.

Subsequent analysis illustrates why the videos remained newsworthy even after official release. For example, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office ([AARO]({{ 'aaro/' | relative_url }})) later assessed that the apparent speed in the *GoFast* video was largely an effect of viewing geometry (parallax) rather than evidence of extraordinary motion, while continuing to catalogue other cases separately. ## What changed in public language and attention

Perhaps TTSA's greatest achievement was changing how the subject was described.

Earlier popular coverage frequently relied on the term "UFO", which carried strong cultural associations with extraterrestrials, conspiracy theories and tabloid entertainment.

Following the 2017 reporting, "unidentified aerial phenomena" (later "unidentified anomalous phenomena") became increasingly common in government documents, military statements and mainstream journalism. The newer terminology encouraged discussion of observed phenomena without implying any particular explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/navy-confirms-ufo-videos-real" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">navy confirms ufo videos real</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Navy Confirms UFO Videos Are Real and Show Unidentified Aerial Phenomena &#124; HISTORYSeptember 19, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2019</span></span></span>

The emphasis also shifted from speculation to process.

Media attention increasingly examined:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* military reporting procedures;
* intelligence oversight;
* aviation safety;
* congressional interest;
* transparency surrounding government investigations.

</div>

This reframing made UAP coverage resemble reporting on defence policy or intelligence oversight rather than paranormal entertainment.

## The limits of TTSA's media success

TTSA's influence on public attention should not be confused with validation of all its broader ambitions.

The organisation promoted ambitious ideas involving advanced materials research and future aerospace technologies that never achieved comparable scientific acceptance. Critics also argued that entertainment, commercial fundraising and extraordinary public claims sometimes blurred the distinction between verified evidence and speculation.

Likewise, the mainstream acceptance of UAP reporting did not produce consensus about what the observed objects actually were. Many analysts continue to argue that individual cases have conventional explanations involving [sensor artefacts]({{ 'sensor-artefacts/' | relative_url }}), atmospheric effects, balloons, aircraft or incomplete data, while others maintain that some military encounters remain unresolved. Official investigations likewise distinguish between identifying unexplained observations and attributing them to any specific cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[aaro.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

## TTSA's enduring media breakthrough

TTSA's lasting contribution was not settling the UFO question but changing the conditions under which it could be discussed.

Its campaign showed that a carefully assembled combination of military evidence, named witnesses, former officials and disciplined media engagement could move UAP reporting into established news organisations. The result was a lasting change in tone: unidentified aerial phenomena became increasingly treated as matters of public policy, military reporting and institutional accountability rather than exclusively as subjects for fringe entertainment.

Whether future investigations ultimately explain most cases conventionally or leave some unresolved, the media transition initiated around TTSA's campaign permanently altered how major news organisations approach the subject.
