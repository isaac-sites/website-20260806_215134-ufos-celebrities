## Introduction

Celebrity pressure did not single-handedly change official UFO policy, but it helped alter the political conditions in which change became possible. The clearest example is Tom DeLonge, the musician whose [To The Stars Academy]({{ 'to-the-stars/' | relative_url }}) brought former defence and intelligence officials into a media-facing organisation and helped place military UAP footage before a mass audience. That publicity contributed to a wider shift: unidentified aerial phenomena were increasingly discussed as aviation-safety, intelligence and government-accountability issues rather than only as entertainment or alien folklore.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufos_and_celebrities_846269_celebrity_advocacy_o_b06026-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Actions" width="1024" height="559" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

The resulting official actions were substantial. The US Navy formalised reporting procedures, Congress ordered intelligence assessments and held public hearings, the Department of Defense created a permanent investigative office, and NASA examined how civilian science could improve data collection. Yet none of those measures established that celebrities’ extraordinary claims were true. Advocacy helped win institutional attention; it did not supply the measurements needed to prove extraterrestrial technology.

## From entertainment coverage to institutional attention

Celebrity UFO advocacy had existed for decades, but most of it operated within entertainment culture: interviews, [documentaries]({{ 'documentaries/' | relative_url }}), convention appearances and personal testimony. DeLonge’s project was different because it deliberately combined celebrity reach with government credentials. To The Stars Academy, launched in 2017, recruited figures including former defence official Christopher Mellon and Luis Elizondo, who said he had worked on a Pentagon effort examining unusual aerial encounters. The organisation packaged their claims, pilot accounts and military videos for journalists and the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. The program, Advanced Aviation Threat Identification Program...</span></span></span>

That structure gave the campaign two forms of authority. DeLonge attracted attention that a retired official or technical specialist might struggle to command alone; his colleagues could present the issue in the language of restricted airspace, sensor data and national security. The most consequential publicity came in December 2017, when reporting on a former Pentagon programme appeared alongside Navy cockpit footage that To The Stars also promoted. The footage did not show enough contextual data to determine what the objects were, and questions remained about the original release process. Nevertheless, it made UAP encounters visible as documented military incidents rather than merely stories told by civilian enthusiasts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/what-is-up-with-those-pentagon-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Up With Those Pentagon UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The article included videos of UAPs and featured accounts from Navy pilots. The distinction mattered. DeLonge often spoke in terms broader and more speculative than government agencies would later endorse, but the public campaign did not need officials to accept an alien explanation to have political impact. It only needed to establish a narrower proposition: trained personnel had recorded objects they could not immediately identify, and the government’s methods for collecting and explaining those observations appeared inconsistent.

By 2019, that narrower framing was appearing in reporting about new Navy procedures and congressional questions. Politicians were asking whether unidentified objects near military operations represented surveillance systems, experimental aircraft, drones, [sensor artefacts]({{ 'sensor-artefacts/' | relative_url }}) or genuine flight hazards. Contemporary coverage explicitly connected some of that attention to material publicised by DeLonge’s organisation and its former-government members.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.vice.com/en/article/tom-delonges-ufo-research-center-is-making-politicians-demand-answers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vice.com">[VICE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vice.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tom Delonge’s UFO Research Center Is Making Politicians Demand Answers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tom Delonge’s UFO Research Center Is Making Politicians Demand AnswersAugust 9, 2019 — Tom Delonge’s UFO Research Center Is Making Po...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 9, 2019</span></span></span>

This does not prove that celebrity campaigning caused the Navy’s decisions. Military aviators, journalists, intelligence officials and members of Congress were independently important. The policy shift is better understood as a coalition effect: celebrity visibility amplified insiders who possessed institutional vocabulary and access, while military encounters gave officials a reason to act without endorsing the most dramatic interpretation.

## Military reporting and congressional interest

One of the most concrete changes was the effort to make UAP reporting more routine. Navy officials acknowledged that personnel needed clearer procedures for documenting unexplained encounters, partly because informal treatment and stigma could prevent potentially useful safety or intelligence information from entering official systems. Coverage of the 2019 policy change described a formal process intended to replace confusion over where and how service members should report an incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/5680192/navy-confirms-ufo-videos-real/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Navy Confirms Existence of &#x27;Unidentified&#x27; Flying Objects Seen in Leaked Footage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Joseph Gradisher, spokesman for the Deputy Chief of Naval Operations, acknowledged the footage as depicting &quot;Unidentified Aerial Phenomen...</span></span></span>

The Department of Defense later widened that approach. A June 2021 memorandum directed military aircrews and government personnel to report UAP observations, called for synchronised collection and analysis, and required reports to reach the UAP task force or its successor within two weeks. The memo justified the work in terms of flight safety, operational security and incursions near defence facilities—not as an investigation premised on alien visitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3100053/dod-announces-the-establishment-of-the-all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on defense.gov.</span></span></span>(https://media.defense.gov/2021/Jun/25/2002749916/-1/-1/1/DEPUTY-SECRETARY-OF-%20DEFENSE-MEMORANDUM-ON-UNIDENTIFIED-AERIAL-PHENOMENA-ASSESSMENTS.PDF)

Congressional involvement gave these administrative changes permanence. A Senate committee directed the intelligence community to produce an assessment of UAP, leading to the Office of the Director of National Intelligence’s June 2021 preliminary report. That assessment was explicitly prepared for Congress and helped establish UAP reporting as a recurring oversight matter rather than a one-off response to media attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/2020/06/22/publications-report-accompany-s-3905-intelligence-authorization-act-fiscal-year-2021-june-17-2020/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[Senate Intelligence Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Committee Report to Accompany S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">3905, The Intelligence Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2021 (June 17, 2020) &#124; Senate Select Committee on IntelligenceJune 22, 2020 — Re...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 17, 2020</span></span></span>

Congress also pushed the Pentagon towards a dedicated organisational structure. In 2022, the Department of Defense established the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, or [AARO]({{ 'aaro/' | relative_url }}), following legislation requiring a broader office to investigate incidents across air, space, maritime and other operating environments. Its remit included collecting reports, coordinating analysis and identifying possible threats to military operations and national security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3100053/dod-announces-the-establishment-of-the-all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on defense.gov.</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3100053/dod-announces-the-establishment-of-the-all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office/)

Public hearings made the shift especially visible. A House Armed Services subcommittee held the first open congressional UFO hearing in more than half a century in 2022. In July 2023, a House Oversight subcommittee heard testimony from former Navy aviators David Fravor and Ryan Graves and former intelligence official David Grusch. The hearing was framed around national security, public safety, reporting mechanisms and government transparency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/release/national-security-subcommittee-to-hold-hearing-on-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena%EF%BF%BC/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

Celebrity advocacy was not the formal basis for those hearings. Witness testimony, defence authorisation legislation, whistleblower allegations and congressional concern about airspace incursions carried more direct institutional weight. Even so, the earlier media campaign helped create an audience for the hearings and a vocabulary through which politicians could discuss the subject without presenting themselves simply as believers in flying saucers.

The 2023 hearing also illustrated both the power and the limits of advocacy. Graves argued that stigma and inadequate procedures discouraged military personnel from filing reports. Members of Congress treated that as a governance problem: underreporting could deprive investigators of information relevant to aviation safety or foreign surveillance. Yet some of the hearing’s most extraordinary claims depended on second-hand accounts or information that was not produced publicly, leaving them difficult to verify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/release/hearing-wrap-up-lack-of-transparency-and-reporting-mechanisms-have-eroded-public-trust-on-governments-handling-of-uap-encounters%EF%BF%BC/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

## What official action actually changed

The practical legacy of the advocacy era is less dramatic than “disclosure”, but more consequential than another round of [celebrity interviews]({{ 'media-gateway/' | relative_url }}). It produced or accelerated several identifiable governance changes:

* **Reporting became a formal duty.** Military personnel received clearer instructions to document unexplained objects rather than dismiss or conceal encounters.
* **UAP data became an intelligence requirement.** Congress required assessments and recurring reports, forcing agencies to aggregate cases that had previously been scattered across commands and departments.
* **A permanent office replaced ad hoc investigation.** AARO was given responsibility for receiving, analysing and resolving reports across several operational domains.
* **Protected reporting channels were introduced.** In 2023, AARO launched a mechanism through which serving and former government personnel and contractors could report alleged UAP-related programmes or activities dating back to 1945.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3100053/dod-announces-the-establishment-of-the-all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on defense.gov.</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/3575511/dod-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-office-launches-new-reporting-tool/)
* **Scientific institutions addressed the data problem.** NASA’s independent study recommended systematic reporting, better-calibrated observations, improved data standards and efforts to reduce stigma.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6a08cbb25fd08" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">uap independent study team final report 0</span></span></span>

These changes should not be confused with official acceptance of celebrity-led theories. A reporting system exists because unidentified objects may include drones, balloons, foreign surveillance systems, atmospheric effects, sensor artefacts or hazards that deserve investigation. “Unidentified” is an initial classification, not a conclusion about origin.

The expanding case totals show why better procedures matter. AARO’s consolidated report covering May 2023 to June 2024 recorded 757 reports, including hundreds submitted after the incidents occurred. Investigators resolved a portion as ordinary objects such as balloons, birds and unmanned aerial systems, while other cases remained open because the available information was insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3100053/dod-announces-the-establishment-of-the-all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on defense.gov.</span></span></span>(https://media.defense.gov/2024/Nov/14/2003583603/-1/-1/0/FY25-CONSOLIDATED-ANNUAL-REPORT-ON-UAP-508.PDF)

An increase in reports therefore does not necessarily indicate an increase in extraordinary craft. It can reflect reduced stigma, clearer instructions and the creation of an office capable of receiving older cases. In policy terms, that is still significant: a system that collects more complete information is better positioned to identify both conventional threats and genuinely anomalous observations.

## Influence without evidential proof

Celebrity advocacy’s strongest demonstrated effect lies in agenda-setting, not scientific discovery. DeLonge and other public figures helped make UAP a subject that major news organisations, elected representatives and serving personnel could discuss openly. Their platforms widened the audience for military witnesses and former officials, while sustained coverage created political incentives for hearings, reports and transparency measures.

But influence is difficult to measure precisely. There is no single document showing that Congress created AARO because a musician demanded it. The policy transition also depended on Navy encounters, internal advocates, journalists, legislators and concern about increasingly capable drones and foreign surveillance. Claims that celebrity pressure directly “forced disclosure” therefore overstate what the documentary record can establish.

There is also a risk that celebrity amplification can outrun verification. Entertainment rewards confident narratives, memorable images and claims of concealed knowledge. Government analysis requires chain of custody, sensor context, metadata, corroboration and plausible alternative explanations. The same publicity that encourages witnesses to report may also encourage audiences to treat an unresolved video as confirmation of a preferred theory.

Official findings have remained much more cautious than the public campaigns surrounding them. AARO has said that it found no empirical evidence that the US government or private companies possess and reverse-engineer extraterrestrial technology. Its historical review also warned that some influential claims had circulated repeatedly among overlapping groups of interviewees, creating an appearance of independent corroboration where the underlying information could be traced to the same small network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Vol_1_2024.pdf?emci=622cd777-b774-f011-8dc9-6045bda9d96b&amp;emdi=ea000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000001&amp;sourceid=1070813" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Unclassified Final DSD AARO Historical Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unclassified Final DSD AARO Historical Report</span></span></span>

NASA reached a similarly restrained position. It regarded UAP as a legitimate data and aviation-safety question but stressed that the available observations were usually too limited to support firm scientific conclusions. Its recommendations centred on rigorous collection, [open data]({{ 'open-data/' | relative_url }}) and calibrated instruments rather than testimony from famous or well-connected individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=6a08cbb25fd08" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">uap independent study team final report 0</span></span></span>

The most accurate verdict is therefore that celebrity advocacy helped change the treatment of the subject, not the evidential standard. It contributed to a political environment in which officials were expected to collect reports, answer congressional questions and investigate incursions rather than ridicule witnesses. That is a genuine policy effect. It does not establish what the unresolved objects were, and it does not turn claims of alien technology into verified fact.

## Did celebrity pressure change official UFO policy?

Yes, but indirectly and only as one part of a wider institutional shift. Celebrity-led campaigns—most notably Tom DeLonge’s work with former defence and intelligence officials—helped transform UAP from an entertainment-centred subject into a visible question of reporting, airspace security and government transparency. That visibility supported the momentum behind Navy procedures, intelligence assessments, hearings and a permanent Pentagon office.

The enduring change was procedural: more reports, clearer channels, greater [congressional oversight]({{ 'congressional-oversight/' | relative_url }}) and less tolerance for simply ignoring unexplained encounters. The unresolved scientific question remained exactly that—unresolved. Celebrity pressure helped persuade institutions to look, record and answer. It did not determine what they would find.
