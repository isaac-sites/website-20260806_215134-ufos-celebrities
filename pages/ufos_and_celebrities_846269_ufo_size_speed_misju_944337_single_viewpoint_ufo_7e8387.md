## Introduction

A single eyewitness cannot determine the exact three-dimensional position of an unidentified object in the sky because the observation provides only a **line of sight**, not a measured distance. This is one of the central reasons why reports of UFOs often contain uncertain estimates of height, size and speed. Whether the witness is a member of the public, a pilot or a celebrity, seeing an object in one direction does not reveal how far away it is. The object could lie almost anywhere along the observer's visual ray unless additional information—such as a second viewpoint, radar data, or a known object crossing in front of or behind it—constrains its location. This geometric limitation is well understood in astronomy, surveying and computer vision, and it also underpins modern guidance on interpreting UAP (Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena) reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AARO_Effect_of_Forced_Perspective_and_Parallax_View_on_UAP_Observations_2024.pdf?ver=WTwJtQaCvC3BkRlBJCGLzg%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[aaro.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP ObservationsMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why landmarks can create false alignment

Witnesses frequently describe a UFO as being "over the trees", "behind the hill", or "next to the Moon". These descriptions are often sincere but can be geometrically misleading.

A photograph or direct observation compresses three-dimensional space into a two-dimensional image. Objects at very different distances can appear perfectly aligned when viewed from one position.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a nearby drone may appear directly above a distant mountain;
* a balloon may seem to hover beside a skyscraper while actually being hundreds of metres closer;
* a bright planet can appear to rest above a roofline despite being millions of kilometres farther away.

</div>

The apparent relationship exists only from that viewing position. Moving a short distance often changes the alignment dramatically, revealing that the objects were never close together in three-dimensional space. This is a classic consequence of perspective geometry rather than evidence that the objects shared the same location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AARO_Effect_of_Forced_Perspective_and_Parallax_View_on_UAP_Observations_2024.pdf?ver=WTwJtQaCvC3BkRlBJCGLzg%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP ObservationsMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

Because many UFO reports involve isolated lights against the night sky, there are often few reliable depth cues available. Without overlapping objects, known-size references or measurable foreground crossings, the visual system has little information from which to infer range.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11512/?report=reader" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Perception of Depth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Webvision - NCBI BookshelfMay 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2005</span></span></span>

## Why one viewpoint leaves distance unresolved

Human vision normally estimates distance using many cues simultaneously, including binocular vision, motion, familiar object sizes and perspective.

Most reported UFOs remove many of those cues:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* they may appear only as points of light;
* they often lack recognisable structure;
* they may be observed against an empty sky;
* they frequently remain visible for only a short time.

</div>

When these cues disappear, the brain naturally fills in missing information using assumptions rather than measurements. This is one reason different witnesses can describe the same object as being both "just above the treetops" and "high above the clouds". Neither estimate can be confirmed from a single viewing position alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11512/?report=reader" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Perception of Depth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Webvision - NCBI BookshelfMay 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2005</span></span></span>

## How multiple viewpoints constrain distance

The geometric ambiguity changes as soon as two independent observation points become available.

If two observers at known locations record the same object at the same moment, each provides a different line of sight. The point where those sight lines intersect—or come closest when measurement uncertainty is included—places strong limits on the object's position.

This is the same geometric principle behind:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* land surveying;
* stereo vision in human eyesight;
* astronomical parallax;
* multi-camera tracking systems.

</div>

Astronomers routinely determine stellar distances by measuring the tiny change in a star's apparent position from two widely separated viewpoints. The underlying mathematics is identical even though the scales are vastly different.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/resource/depth-perception-in-space/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Depth Perception in Space</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceDepth Perception in Space - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

## Why movement alone is not enough

Some witnesses assume that tracking an object's motion automatically reveals its distance. In reality, following a target from one position usually traces only a changing direction over time.

Unless the observer's own position changes in a measured way—or another observer contributes an independent angle—the object may still occupy many possible trajectories in three-dimensional space.

For example, a light appearing to travel horizontally across the sky could represent:

* a nearby object moving slowly;
* a distant aircraft moving much faster;
* or an object travelling partly towards or away from the observer.

These different paths can project almost identical motion onto the observer's field of view. [AARO]({{ 'aaro/' | relative_url }}) notes that perspective effects and parallax can significantly distort perceived direction, speed and size, particularly when reports rely on a single moving observer or sensor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AARO_Effect_of_Forced_Perspective_and_Parallax_View_on_UAP_Observations_2024.pdf?ver=WTwJtQaCvC3BkRlBJCGLzg%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP ObservationsMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why this matters when assessing UFO reports

This geometric limitation does not imply that witnesses are dishonest or that every report has a conventional explanation. Instead, it defines what can and cannot be inferred from the available evidence.

A lone eyewitness can often provide valuable information about:

* the object's appearance;
* colour;
* brightness;
* duration;
* apparent motion;
* and changes over time.

However, that testimony alone cannot uniquely determine the object's three-dimensional position. Consequently, claims that an observed UFO was extremely close, enormous or travelling at extraordinary speed remain difficult to verify unless independent measurements establish its distance.

For this reason, modern UAP investigations place particular value on observations that combine multiple viewpoints or independent sensor systems, allowing investigators to replace visual assumptions with measurable geometry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AARO_Effect_of_Forced_Perspective_and_Parallax_View_on_UAP_Observations_2024.pdf?ver=WTwJtQaCvC3BkRlBJCGLzg%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of Forced Perspective and Parallax View on UAP ObservationsMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>
